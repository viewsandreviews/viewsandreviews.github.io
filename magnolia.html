Film Review

Magnolia

An Ever Blossoming Marvel of Perseverance and Regret
Director Paul Thomas Anderson
Year 1999
Starring Tom Cruise, Julianne Moore, Philip Seymour Hoffman, John C. Reilly

A symphony of torment, suffering and regret,
with crescendos of indelible human perseverance,
pursuits of love and forgiveness.

After being left with a tepid, soupy taste in my mouth by Wes Anderson's latest film, The French Dispatch, I decided to revisit my favourite movie by my favourite Anderson. No, Paul W. S. Anderson, not you. You're not even your wife's favourite Anderson.

Honestly, it was actually quite the challenge to select which of Paul Thomas Anderson's movies I'd rather review first, as I wait to take a bite out of his upcoming flick: Licorice Pizza. Then I remembered which of his movies has my favourite quote of all time.

✦

One of the issues I had with The French Dispatch is the same issue I have with most anthologies. You take a bunch of stories, often with alarming fluctuations in quality, speed run character and story development and hope one or two stick to the brain walls of viewers.

This means that even in the best case, which is when one of the short films is captivating enough to awaken the viewer, we have to immediately discard lingering onto that pleasurable moment to be forcefully fed an all new platter.

That's why the anthology expedient works much better for the horror genre, where any type of development is just an excuse to cover up fetishes of seeing space leprechauns slashing n' dashing horny teens lost in a cabin. Wes Anderson was not the only one of the great names to recently produce lackluster anthologies. One of my all-time favourites, the Coen Bros., also couldn't quite achieve their usual levels of merit in The Ballad of Buster Scruggs.

Magnolia, on the other hand, is what I consider an anthology done right. Possibly the only anthology done right.

✦

Just like a beautiful, fast-paced blossoming marvel, Magnolia is as good to the eyes as it is to the ears, thanks to Aimee Mann's iconic tracks.

The main difference between Magnolia (and Pulp Fiction) to the above mentioned anthologies are many, but the one that stands out the most is undoubtedly their avoidance of creating separate narrations, opting instead for a more continuous, albeit skillfully intermittent storytelling.

Magnolia sees the stories of a stellar ensemble cast intertwine for the duration of a day; a day of biblical providence. It all starts with an intriguing narration on the absurdity of real-life coincidences, which definitely helps glue together what is about to come to the central fulcrum.

✦

It's hard to even pinpoint any particular highlight. All of the shorts and characters live together harmoniously, for however absurdly distinct and captivating each may be.

A romantic, dutiful, albeit incompetent policeman (John C. Reilly); the most aggressively charismatic and intimidating real-life pick-up artist (Tom Cruise); a cocaine-addicted, sexual PTSD victim (Melora Walters) and her sleazy TV show presenter father (Philip Baker Hall); and finally a neurotic, cheating but remorseful trophy wife (Julianne Moore). These are just some of the bigger-than-life but creepily relatable characters designed by PTA.

Moore's and Cruise's performances stand out in a sea of talent, for diagonally opposite reasons. The usually tranquil and sober Moore gives an out-of-range, eccentric performance, which ends up being the equivalent of a drag queen at an orthodox Jewish seminar, making for some bizarrely hilarious moments.

Cruise instead gives a once-in-a-lifetime performance, fully capturing the eerie charisma of PUAs and showcasing a ripe emotional range in the more dramatic scenes. Too bad Ron "Jubba the" Hubbard stole any chances of Cruise ever getting an Oscar.

✦

His drum-like, rhapsodic performance is the main key in PTA's symphony. A symphony of torment, suffering and regret, with crescendos of indelible human perseverance, pursuits of love and forgiveness.

The biggest merit of Magnolia remains being able to navigate so many tales, without ever getting lost in the dark forests of narrative convolution. All those tales, tangled like a Japanese school girl stuck in the wrong train ride, slowly come together to form a circular thread.

A thread I hope you all pick apart and adorn your current movie collection with. Hey, it smells pretty good at the very least.
