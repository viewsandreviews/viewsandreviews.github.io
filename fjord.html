Film Review

Fjord

Even in Paradise, Someone Is Knocking on Your Door
Director Cristian Mungiu
Year 2024
Starring Sebastian Stan, Renate Reinsve

There is a version of this story that has already been told.
It was told in 2023, in Hindi, with considerably more screaming.
It scored 13% on Rotten Tomatoes.
It was the wrong film for a complicated story.

It was called Mrs. Chatterjee vs Norway, and it painted the Norwegian child welfare system with the same broad, accusatory brush that Bollywood reserves for its most satisfying villains. Norway was the bad guy. India was the heart. The moral was delivered at volume, in case you missed it the first three times.

Cristian Mungiu — the Romanian director who has built an entire career out of refusing to let complicated stories be simple ones — has now made the right one.

✦

Fjord begins where most films of its kind would end: with a family that looks, on the surface, like it should be fine.

Mihai and Lisbet Gheorghiu have moved to Lisbet's remote Norwegian hometown with their children, their faith, and the quiet confidence of two people who believe they are doing everything correctly. They are conservative, religious, devoted parents. They are also, by the precise standards of the progressive Scandinavian community surrounding them, doing several things differently.

When the Norwegian child welfare services come knocking, what begins as a question of miscommunication metastasises, with the slow inevitability of a Mungiu film, into something that will define the rest of their lives.

✦

What Mungiu does, with Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve, is find two performers capable of carrying the film's considerable weight in near silence. Their quiet, steady strength is where Fjord lives at its best — two people who have been trained by faith and circumstance to absorb rather than explode, and who are now being asked to absorb something that has no bottom.

Stan in particular, playing Mihai with a Romanian stoicism that feels earned rather than performed, is doing the kind of work that doesn't announce itself and doesn't need to.

The teenagers, however, are a different matter entirely. While the Norwegian teenage actor's market may have its limitations, and I try not to fault them for not being included in the top 1% of worldwide performers, I can blame Mungiu for creating them as unpolished characters — sketched in rather than filled out, left in a draft state of idealistic standing that the script never quite gets around to animating. In a film this precise about everything else, it registers.

✦

One of the great strengths of Fjord — and its main upper hand over Chatterjee's Looney Tunes misadventures — is that Mungiu refuses to hand you a detestable villain.

The Norwegian system is not cartoonishly cruel. The Gheorghius are not cartoonishly innocent. Everyone in this film is operating from a position of genuine conviction, which is precisely what makes it so unbearable to watch. Cartoons are easy. Mungiu doesn't do easy.

That being said, Mungiu, for all his moral evenhandedness, does eventually pick a winner. Not loudly. Not crudely. But there comes a point in Fjord where the camera's sympathy settles, and some of the supporting characters — particularly on the Norwegian institutional side — stop being people and start being positions.

It is not a fatal flaw. It is, however, a noticeable one in a film that had spent two hours earning the right to have no positions at all.

✦

What stays with you, though, is something considerably larger than its flaws.

Fjord understands something that Mrs. Chatterjee — and most films about cultural conflict — fundamentally misses: that when a loud majority becomes a loud minority, the pressure of social dynamics doesn't disappear. It is simply shuffled.

The Gheorghius are not persecuted because Norway is evil. They are ground down because systems designed for consensus have no framework for the person who stands outside it, and no particular interest in developing one.

It reminds you that even in the most idyllic of places — and Hjørundfjorden is achingly, almost obscenely beautiful — conflict exists. And what might have been resolved as a misunderstanding becomes, through the accumulated weight of institutional certainty and cultural distance, a life-defining tragedy.

Mungiu films it all with the patience of someone who knows that the most devastating things rarely announce themselves.

They just keep happening, quietly, until they don't.
