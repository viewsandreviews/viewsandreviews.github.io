TV Review

The Night Of

A Slow-Motion Autopsy of Faith in Law, in Fairness, in Self-Judgement
Network HBO
Year 2016
Starring Riz Ahmed, John Turturro

The night of a crime is often the night of a mystery.
That is, if you are an HBO exec.
The night of a mystery is often the Night Of a crime.
That is, if you are of an exotic complexion.

What The Night Of truly is, is a slow-motion autopsy of faith — in law, in fairness, in self-judgement.

Naz Khan (Riz Ahmed) begins as every immigrant parent's fantasy: polite, hardworking, pre-med. Then one bad night, one bad girl, one bad decision — and he's swallowed whole. The crime may be ambiguous, but the punishment is efficient.

✦

Riz Ahmed plays the timid good-doer with terrifying precision. As the episodes reel in, you realize he's not really being judged for murder, but for daring to exist in a country that still sees his skin as probable cause — only to later realize you didn't know him in the very first place.

That's also when you take in Jack Stone's (John Turturro) extravagant determination. He knows how this story ends. He's seen it a hundred times — the innocent (?) accused, the guilty (?) rewarded, justice traded for convenience.

But what he learned before we ever could is that innocence and guilt are not axes — they're resolve and twists of fate, battling to make it out on top while a jury of distracted viewers watches. That's also what makes him trudge forward, like a man addicted to disappointment, still with a shining hope to see the invisible made known.

✦

Visually, the series feels like a crime scene frozen mid-decay. The lighting is jaundiced, the air heavy, the city indifferent. It's not filmed — it's quarantined.

Every frame reminds you that truth doesn't survive contact with bureaucracy. And then, somewhere in all the paperwork, the moral compass dies quietly. Cops bend the rules. Lawyers bend the facts. Naz bends himself into whatever shape the world demands to stay alive.

✦

The who-did-it chases, typical of the genre, lose focus on the who or why — only to come back with double the force, after taking us on a moral trial of our own.

Who did it mutates into who is Naz. An all-too-curated initial framing of our protagonist is soon twisted only to reveal that we ourselves did the curating. No misleading; no lies.

The lesson is not that he may not be who we were introduced to. It is for us to realize we were never introduced to him in the first place.

✦

That's The Night Of's unbearable brilliance — making us aware of our own assumptions with the egregious talent pool only afforded by HBO's hefty pockets.

Ultimately, there's no big reveal, no villain to hate, no redemption to celebrate. Just the steady grind of systems pretending to function. It's a story without resolution because the real world rarely grants one.

What it does grant is a plethora of great crime thrills — and thrills of crimes that may never have been.
