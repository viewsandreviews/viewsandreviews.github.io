Film Review

One Battle After Another

The Revolution Will Be Tele-Fucking-Vised
Director Paul Thomas Anderson
Year 2025
Starring Leonardo DiCaprio, Teyana Taylor, Sean Penn

Guess what William Wallace —
the revolution will be, in the very matter of fact,
tele-fucking-vised.
And who else to do it than the capital PTA.

One Paul Thomas Anderson whose return has been awaited — and who returns to bring both old and late idiosyncrasy. What comes back isn't quite the PTA we've known in the last decade; neither is it the pizza-munching subversionist of the late 90s. Instead we get the sweeping, neurotic chaos of his sprouting thoughts, molded into the beautiful threads of visual storytelling he has now mastered.

✦

One battle after another. That is the story that characterizes his ensemble.

First in command: Leonardo DiCaprio as Bob Ferguson, a former revolutionary turned reluctant dad. He lives off-grid with his daughter Willa (Chase Infiniti), trying to bury his past in paranoia and weed. Leo turns his biggest paternal nightmare into fuel to catch academy gold — gold he will most likely have to share with one of his co-stars.

Left in Bob's past is his ex-lover Perfidia (Teyana Taylor), and her sexually repressed gimp, Colonel Steven J. Lockjaw — played by Sean Penn, who brings his years of predator method acting into the scene. And scenes he steals in abundance.

✦

But rarity is not past that infiltrates present. As the previous life of Bob comes to haunt him, and all he holds dear, an old battle — lost in time and forgotten motives — shakes the same old war drawboards.

The plot unfolds in dual timelines: a blood-rushing first half-hour immerses us in the fiery days of uprising, propaganda, and idealism; then the tense present, where forgiveness is optional and retribution is inevitable. The stakes feel increasingly, claustrophobically high — kidnappings, political cults with untimely Christmas motifs, undercover power plays — but Anderson always keeps things human. Zealously human.

✦

The pacing feels at times an oddity. But what starts as confusion over apparent mistiming transforms into excitement.

The story is revealing itself not in the way it wants to be told, but in the way it wants to be seen. That, together with the abundant juxtapositions in characterization built up by Anderson, creates an aura of allure — a fire match for thoughts.

Taylor's Perfidia burns like gasoline: she demands attention, for her cause and for her ego. Bob — the out-of-place militant, the out-of-place dad — is still always in place to respond to the callings he is made responsible for. Lockjaw: the militant ultra-nationalist with a case of triple-A jungle fever.

What more could one ask. To be answered for questions never made.

✦

Visually, the film is Wichita meets dystopia — terrain both familiar and unhinged. Cinematography snaps from intimate father-daughter moments to full-tilt riots without losing its grip.

Jonny Greenwood's score does miracles in keeping things within the feverous box of angst Anderson sets up. Hums and jolts — a thump under the skin — hearts that keep racing between scenes of heartbreak and absurd comedy.

✦

What we come to accept by the end is that One Battle After Another — and PTA by extension — doesn't ask for approval. It demands attention. It laughs at cynicism, then punches you in the heart.

In a year full of shadows, it reminds us: battles don't end just because you wish them so. Sometimes you fight because there are no other songs to sing.

Only more dust to bite.
