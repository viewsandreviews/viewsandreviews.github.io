TV Review

Succession

Half the 100, Still Left with Curtis Jackson
Creator Jesse Armstrong
Years 2018–2023
Starring Brian Cox, Jeremy Strong, Sarah Snook, Kieran Culkin

In the words of Queens native and modern-day Aristotle,
Curtis "6 kroner" Jackson:
pursue riches, or die trying.

But truth is, some people are born into riches — and even in death, they're wrapped in silky garments, laid down in future wonders of the world. Some call these people the overlords, others the reptile gang-gang; few even call them by their name. They have been here since the beginning of time. Sometimes we fear them, envy them; throw our fists in their direction, always making sure to avoid any contact. Nowadays though, we enjoy laughing at them, and even pitying them. We do so because we are them, for the most part.

The missing link being the 30+ billion at stake in this HBO-styled soap opera. That is my un-Googled estimate of Logan Roy & Co.'s family stake, star of this show. But this is no family of Dominic Toretto — the Roy family exists only by name.

✦

A patriarchal monarchy of its own, governing a not-so-fictional media landscape — that is Logan Roy's prize jewel. The centrepiece of his crown of money and power, adorned by smaller, almost frivolous side pieces he calls children.

Kendall, Roman, Shiv and the ever-forgotten Connor. A quartet that, again, exists only by name, and to continue Logan Roy's mission of perma-world dominance. A 20-page essay could be written about each of them and still manage to miss some of their nuances, far too well threaded by the showrunners. I thus instead present a three-adjective summary of each, finally satisfying the wishes of my middle school English teacher.

✦

The story follows the battles for the succession to the throne of King Lear Roy. A battle from which he hopes a victor will emerge through sheer willpower and carnage. That is the way of Logan Roy — a man who suffered and decided to never suffer again. He now holds the whip to the world. A whip that came with a burden that only he is able to carry, unfortunately.

It is early found that none of his heirs is adequate enough to him to continue this American dynasty. For they are people, or at least more so than he ever could be. A humanity he had left long ago is still very well attached to all the mini Roys, in the good and the terrible. But the rules of human life are still bound to Logan, and that notion is all too much to handle.

✦

The ever-serious family diatribe is alternated wisely with appearances by the comedic cornerstone of the show: the delinquent duo, the disgusting brothers. The sycophant Tom Wambsgans and human inflatable wacky waving man, cousin Greg. Starting as the exponentially dumber Pinky and the Brain of the series, what are meant to be comic relief evolve into monsters of their own. But they are certainly not the only ones — plenty of time is given to all of the many excellent characters to be fully fleshed out.

In the end, they know themselves better than we give them credit for, more than we could ever do. Almost too well to be credible, but just not enough to remove the figure from fiction.

✦

The loudest praise that must be given to the show is its ability to reconnect the disconnect between the 0.01% and the other percent. Well, maybe not reconnect — as the Roys would revolt at the idea of ever descending to the animal kingdom again. But that barrier that makes it so visible for the ultra-rich to remain invisible is magically, and beautifully, turned off.

By the end, Succession ends up as a masterclass of everything. The Henry Ford of working. The Titanic of grandiosity. Truly the best of the best. The only melancholy that remains is the suffering of knowing that, just like the dynasties it explores, it will inevitably come to an end.

But from the ashes of human depravity, a phoenix of talent is bound to re-emerge. And for that we wait — us measly humans, served by the true gods of the silver screen.
