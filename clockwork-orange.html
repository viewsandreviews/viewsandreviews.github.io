Film Review

A Clockwork Orange

Review #10: The Otto & Quattro Mezzi
Director Stanley Kubrick
Year 1971
Starring Malcolm McDowell, Patrick Magee, Michael Bates

The movie that impacted yours truly like no other movie ever did.
An orange I never stopped peeling, 'cause it smells oh so good.

Review number Ten. Numero Dieci. The big One Zero.

Honestly, with ten being such an iconic number, I always struggled to understand the convention of 8 being the hallmark for movie directors. From Otto e mezzo to The Hateful Eight, it seems that our fascination with the inexplicable often likes to take a very orderly, formulaic form.

That's why I meticulously planned to review what was the most unconventional, chaotic and inspiring movie I had seen in my ripe adolescence as my big number ten, my Otto & Quattro Mezzi.

I'm talking about a movie... forgive me... The movie that awakened the realization of how incredibly powerful of a medium films can be. Not just because of their convenience as a low effort bait for promiscuous encounters, neither for their boner inducing properties, that's a totally different ball game.

✦

That being said, promiscuity and its implications were undoubtedly one of the central revelations that came out of this ticking orange; an orange that I never stopped peeling, 'cause it smells oh so good.

I'm talking about the one and only A Clockwork Orange. The movie with the biggest recurring generational impact. The movie that impacted yours truly like no other movie ever did.

I won't go into details on what follows in the plot. I don't think I could do justice to that with my incessant need for attention via confusing wordplay and B tier humour.

Instead, I will detail what and how this mighty marvel by the nigh-flawless Kubrick violently stole all of my innocence and transformed me into a morally ambivalent monster. Just kidding, possibly. Well, maybe not about the ambivalent part.

✦

See, I too was once just a moderately narcissistic teen pursuing a life of normative hedonism. A self-indulging Robin Hood of utilitarianism; an intellectually-impaired Epicurean. A jovial, free-spirited child, wandering the seclusive walls of a traditionally conservative household, without any religious or societal attachment, nor guidance. Nonetheless, with a strong sense of pursuit of knowledge, self-understanding, and more than anything else, of pleasure.

Don't worry; I wasn't Alex DeLarge; the deranged, sinful and ethically apathetic protagonist of this beautiful orange. At the very least, I knew I didn't want to be.

I didn't want to be a rape-loving goon, a violence-prone thug, an all-consuming, nothing-giving punk. I didn't want to be Alex. I didn't want to be a monster.

And still, I felt like I understood him. His desires, his passions, his pursuits. What I was afraid of — as many in the same paradigm are and should be — was that I couldn't argue myself to an absolute certainty that who he was and what he did was empirically wrong.

✦

I mean, of course rape is wrong. Of course gang violence and psychopathic behaviour is unacceptable. Of course. But that's what occurred. A lack of dogmatism or other pillars of morality and ethics to hold on to brought me to wonder what is that makes things "of course right" and "of course wrong". Even as a late supporter of techno-progressivism, I couldn't stop but think: why?

Yes, all those past-times of Alex are definitely in opposition to the democratic process and to human liberties. But democracy failed us since the beginning of time and there's no such thing as true liberty. We are nothing but monsters, or the victims, the pawns of said monsters. Or some bullshit like that. Listen, I was 15, without any guidance and just started my first philosophy classes. Of course I was edgier than a Miracle blade.

✦

But in truth, we are often at the mercy of these monsters. And I too was poised in the uncertainty of whether it was these monsters that shaped the world we wander in, or the opposite — whether it was this same world that manufactured these frightening, but all so powerful monsters.

So why shouldn't we be one?

If you're looking for an answer, well you won't find one here. This is a movie review after all. But I'm glad to say that I have found my answer, and that A Clockwork Orange pushed me to that uncomfortable limit.

✦

That's what I believe made A Clockwork Orange so resonating at its time of release and what continues to make it so resonating. The way it so powerfully captured the uncertainty of our most susceptible years. An uncertainty that is often ignored or shelved by the individual, and even more so by the ever-shifting world of man-made and man-purposed morality.

What was once patched up by religious dogmatism and survival necessities is not sufficient to the man of tomorrow. A man who knows more than they ever did, a man who can do more than it was ever done, and a man who craves more than was ever needed.

Now, with the intervention of papa Frank, I will stop my ticking self-indulgent clock and urge you to watch the much worthier A Clockwork Orange before your ticking curiosity comes to an end.
