Enter the Void

Debilitated and Liberated. Death Is One Hell of a Drug.
Director Gaspar Noé
Year 2009
Starring Nathaniel Brown, Paz de la Huerta, Cyril Roy

We live, we die, we crave.
Whichever the order of these may be,
we should not be ashamed of it, nor run away from it.

There is a reason if the term provocateur has infiltrated the Oxford dictionary unaltered; those French perverts just do it better. And Gaspar Noé may very well be the king Charles VI of French perverts. Not because of their shared mentally ill mind — I mean, not just that. Mentally ill mind it is.

But for those brave missionaries patient enough to climb over the wall of provocation set by Noé, they may find a purpose; a spiritual essence in his madness.

That is why, encouraged by my commitment to the scientific method, I decided to revisit Noé's best work after a considerable dose of the best fungi Norway had to offer (for all purposes, people and events cited in this article are a work of fiction and fiction only).

This fictional experience I so courageously took part in indubitably helped connect the many previously incongruent, directionless thoughts floating both in the void of my brain and the void presented in the movie.

✦

It all starts with the alive and unwell Oscar, a drug dealer who clearly didn't get the moral lesson of Scarface: do not get high on your own supply.

Oscar is shown as a little speck of nothingness in a punk dystopian Tokyo, full of oxy-morons. This neon flytrap of a city that he calls home is both claustrophobically full of anxiety and bleakness, while also evoking feelings of interminable emptiness.

Consumed by his criminal struggles, Oscar meets an early end at the 25th-minute mark of the movie. The end. But wait, there's more. A lot more; a bit too much more.

✦

Oscar is now a vagrant spirit, in its most irreligious form. Unwielding all his mortal necessities, the now unshapely Oscar is freely roaming past and present. But just like a DMT-infused Gautama, his emancipation from mortality has yet to end his pursuit of carnal desires. The loop is not over.

Together with the memories of his past, we relive his innermost longings; his unfulfilled dreams.

Noé is able to majestically correlate the willful state of liberation and vulnerability of an acid trip to its death phantasium. Which makes sense, if you believe his claims that the brain releases traceable amounts of DMT post-mortem.

✦

This psychedelic death trip we're obliged to partake in together with Oscar leaves the higher questions unanswered. Actually, it doesn't even bother pretending to care answering them; that's what drugs are for.

In spirit, Noé leaves us no control of time and dr(a/u)gs us into a crescendo of unfiltered and logorrheic imagery, which lasts for an unneeded two and a half extra hours. Time jumps, overlong shots, Noé's trademarks of sexual taboo, violence and trippiness can feel like a bit of a nauseating nuisance. But here is where the fungi kicks in.

Those who dared to do what shall not be named may start to find an understanding into all this chaos. Whether this new awareness makes sense or not is completely trivial — all that matters is that now we're able to assign some emotional significance to it.

✦

Enter the Void is an unclear reminder of our struggles, our fears, our needs, our purpose. We live, we die, we crave. Whichever the order of these may be, we should not be ashamed of it, nor run away from it. It all makes sense, I think.

And now that I slowly realize I am turning this into an overly verbose manifesto of my inflated ego, out of respect for you all, unlike Noé, I shall call it quits. Now go ahead and enjoy the rest of your journey, and please do not die on my lawn.
