Film Review

Obsession

Aventura
Director Curry Barker
Year 2026
Starring Michael Johnston, Inde Navarrette

"Be careful what you wish for" — dyslexic genie after giving me a 12 inch pianist.

The Horror-aissance continues.

Every new calendar year since the long gone Babadook-y 2010s seems to be a winning year for the spooky genre. Budget restraints. Audience group limitations. There truly doesn't seem to be anything that can stop 'em. One could even say that these constraints are what give them strength, as the ever increasing new batch of fresh Horror creators makes of challenge a creativity virtue.

Spooky times truly bring in spooky cravings. Obsessing, even.

✦

Unmanaged cravings is also what Curry Barker's big screen debut goes into to so deliciously unwrap. Written, directed, and edited by the man himself — presumably because he trusts absolutely no one, least of all himself — Obsession follows Bear, a music store employee whose unrequited crush on his friend Nikki reaches the kind of fever pitch that, in any reasonable timeline, ends with a barrage of posts on r/incels.

Instead, Bear finds a cursed willow tree. Breaks it. Wishes Nikki would love him. It works.

Horror ensues.

Yes, the premise is as original as every other populist's political campaign, and in every other case it should not work as well as it does, especially on a budget that wouldn't cover Kim Jong Un's barber expenses.

And yet here we are: $220+ million box office, a Blumhouse production that genuinely earns its scares, and a central metaphor so simple it's almost embarrassing nobody made this movie sooner.

Until you realize they did. Many, many times.

✦

From the wish-granting curse, to the evermore popular out-of-your-league obsessed girl trope. Where Barker's spice blend truly delights is not in his storytelling creativity, but in his ability to wring genuine discomfort, and more importantly genuine laughs, out of a premise you've already seen, felt, and probably lived through in some embarrassing iteration of your own adolescence.

The familiarity isn't a bug. It's the whole feature. You recognise Bear because you've met him. You may have, at one particularly regrettable juncture, been him.

Michael Johnston plays Bear with the perfect "nice guy" energy — the kind that sits, perfectly balanced, on the fence ranging from sympathetic enough to keep giving him the benefit of the doubt, right up until the moment you shouldn't. Until you do again.

Inde Navarrette channels her inner Carrie while bringing some Gen Z flare. Her Nikki begins as the object of the film's obsession and slowly, uncomfortably, becomes its conscience — a girl who didn't ask to be loved this hard and has to spend the entire runtime paying for someone else's wish. It's a difficult needle to thread and she lands it without breaking a sweat, which is either great acting or extremely concerning self-control.

✦

The dialogue between the late teens moves with the fluidity and genuineness of the great teen movies à la Superbad, minus the evergreen nicknames.

McLovin, a man to truly obsess about.

That, together with superb cinematography that wrings maximum unease out of minimum resources, makes for the kind of horror-comedy formula that studios keep trying to manufacture and indie directors keep accidentally stumbling into by just making something real.

Barker jumps into the scene with the same energetic bonanza as a 2022 Zach Cregger — that specific brand of directorial confidence that convinces you someone has planned ten moves ahead, even when what's on screen looks deceptively simple.

He earns his scares the old fashioned way: by making you care first, then making you regret it.

The result is a film that understands, perhaps better than most, that the most unsettling horror doesn't come from what the monster does to you.

It comes from recognising yourself in the person who let it in.

So celebrate, Barker, as I as many other crown you as the King of 2026 Horror.

Your move, Cregger.
