Film Review

The Drama

Pumped Up Kirks
Director Kristoffer Borgli
Year 2026
Starring Robert Pattinson, Zendaya

"What is the worst thing you have ever done?" — She asked.
"I paid for Grok Super." — He answered.
"Bang Bang." — She shot.

Now. The Drama.

Directed by Kristoffer Borgli — the Norwegian gentleman who reminds the international public of the cartoonish levels of social awkwardness that only Scandinavian minds can conceive — The Drama is about a very simple premise: what do you do when the person you're about to marry turns out to contain a secret so spectacularly terrible that your brain short-circuits and you begin making catastrophically bad decisions in rapid succession?

The answer, as delivered by one Robert Pattinson, is: you make it into the Drama; the Harlequin kind.

A quick note. Yours truly and his recently lawfully elected wife had to postpone the viewing of said Drama to after their special day. That was, to say the least, a fortunate coincidence.

✦

Now, Pattinson. Roberto. We are aware you have a bigger range than sparkly vampire with permanent pre-orgasm face syndrome. You do not have to prove it to us by playing every variation of unstable character imaginable.

From feral lighthouse keeper to sad Batman and now this — a man named Charlie who, upon learning his fiancée's darkest secret, responds with the emotional intelligence of a parrot who's been living exclusively in Epstein Island's massage room. Brilliant.

His counterpart is Zendaya. Emma. Who is asked by the script to carry something considerably heavier than Pattinson's chaos.

Emma is not a villain. Emma is not a victim. Emma is a person who did something terrible at fifteen, rebuilt herself around it, and now has to watch her wedding week collapse because one dinner party game had no emergency exit.

Luckily Emma is very familiar with the gaming concept of Quick Saving before boss fights. You lose, you learn, you re-start.

✦

Where the film truly earns its keep — beyond the performances, beyond the wickedly dry humour of watching a wedding reception achieve the social dynamics of a UN peacekeeping failure — is in what it has to say about how we process other people's darkness.

Charlie doesn't fail Emma because of what she did. He fails her because he was never prepared to find out that people he loves contain things he can't immediately categorise.

If there's a complaint to be made, it's that it centralizes that confession as the be all or lose it-all of their relationship, as well as the whole movie itself. Which is fine, albeit slightly numbing; as even the European mind has now been numbed by the American tradition of school fireworks, and their posthumous celebrations.

✦

Do I recommend it? Yes, with the caveat that you should not watch it with your partner on the night before a difficult conversation you've been avoiding.

I speak from no experience whatsoever [citation needed].
