Film Review

Perfect Days

Are Never Over
Director Wim Wenders
Year 2023
Starring Kōji Yakusho

Sometimes, to find that inner peace,
a balance of acceptance and forgettance
has to be reached.

Wim Wenders. A name that causes great rhythmic envy to even Wes Anderson. He is the German face that brings back an old alliance with the Eastern front — one that, for now, has quite different goals and outcomes. The product, not as explosive, is a delicate slice of life. One that the Japanese have made us acquainted with through their numerous animated productions. One that sometimes is still animated through brilliant human performances.

✦

The story is that of Hirayama (Kōji Yakusho). A well-kept, van-riding janitor, spending his senior days in a routine that can be appreciated by very few. He lives in a not-so-bustling Tokyo — a Tokyo that is rarely filmed. One where bright lights do not often shine, aside from a beautiful sun whose rays penetrate the forests and parks he often likes to photograph.

Botany and reading are his other hobbies, both cultivated between his one-person-sized apartment, his favourite spots in the city, and his van. His van is also where he enjoys his American and British oldies, many of which carry an intense soul rhythm — one he savours for himself and his simple life. That is his day. Rinse and repeat. Perfection.

✦

The often taciturn Hirayama is joined on screen by the seldom appearances of younger co-stars — one in his good-for-nothing coworker, and later his niece. Both are people of words; full of questions and wonders. People who have just learned the different pains that life provides; people who still look for answers.

The true spirit of the Japanese generational diaspora is captured by the very non-native Wenders — captured so well that an answer is given. An objective answer, one that can rarely be argued with. An answer that goes by the name of time. And perspective. That is how Hirayama has been able to find his inner peace, although it is an unfortunately fragile peace.

✦

He wakes up every day, with Lynchian intermissions of a present that is to be experienced and sometimes dreamt about. But those visions are often embroiled with shards of a hurtful past. For as much as he tries to forget, the perfect day is often a mirage. But what is a mirage if not a dream that is so close — one that is clear, but still looks one step too far?

What Wenders provides is one of those not-so-rare slow burners, capable of enflaming hearts as the days wrap up and the tears start flowing. He is an old soul who still goes forward. And Kōji Yakusho does an excellent job at capturing the director's intent. A mind that meditates can become a strong mind. A mind that meditates too long can turn into a lost mind. The zen teachings are brought to the surface with a simple, delicate touch of screenwriting and shooting. The story goes forward, as does life within it. And as it goes forward, new answers are found.

✦

The last moments, guided by a powerful Nina Simone, wrap up the director's thesis on the power of music. Those beautiful small windows to memory lane. Nostalgia. Sorrow. Joy. Now we're feeling good.

The perfect day is ultimately nothing more than a sweet and sour source, for a palate that has learned the pleasure of modesty — and Asian persuasion.
