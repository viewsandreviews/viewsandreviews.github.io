Film Review

A Complete Unknown

(Never) Inside Llewyn Dylan
Director James Mangold
Year 2024
Starring Timothée Chalamet, Elle Fanning, Edward Norton

Bob Dylan is an enigma.
A Complete Unknown is not his Alan Turing.
A riddle wrapped in a harmonica solo.
A poet who mumbled his way into legend.

Making a biopic about Dylan is like trying to paint fog — you can capture the shape, the movement, but never quite the essence. Enter A Complete Unknown, a film that doesn't so much dissect Dylan as let him float through the frame: an observer in his own myth.

What could have been a semi-diluted Inside Llewyn Davis instead becomes something stranger — or more accurately, far too standardly competent. A movie about folk music as much as it is about the man who helped drag it, kicking and wailing, into history.

✦

Dylan isn't always the star of his own show. He often takes a backseat — literally and figuratively — to the world of folk that surrounds him. The film is alive with music, overflowing with the voices, venues, and chaos that gave birth to the movement. It doesn't feel like a Bob Dylan Story™ so much as an ensemble piece where Dylan just happens to be loitering in the background, occasionally stepping forward to remind you that, yes, he's the main event.

When he does take the stage, though, it's with that signature Dylan swagger — part prophet, part trickster, all contradiction. Timothée Chalamet, in a move that can only be described as "method acting with better cheekbones," nails the mannerisms, the crooked smirk, the casual aloofness of a man who spent his whole life running from the idea of himself.

But the mystery? That ineffable, untouchable thing that makes Dylan Dylan? It slips through Chalamet's fingers. He's a perfect imitation, but the soul remains just out of reach. It's not his fault — even Dylan couldn't play Dylan half the time.

✦

The first half moves like a well-worn folk ballad, letting the music tell the story, capturing the raw, unpolished magic of the era. But when the soundtrack fades, things start to feel… procedural. The energy dips. The reason for Dylan's explosion — why he, out of all the guitar-wielding rebels, became the one — gets lost in the haze.

And then there's the pacing, which somehow manages to be both too slow and too fast. Non-folk fans might find themselves checking their watches, wondering why so much time is spent on smoky Greenwich Village bars and half-finished verses. Meanwhile, folk purists will likely grumble that the film breezes through too many songs, reducing what could have been an intricate anthology into a mere highlight reel. A whole movement, compressed into a ballad.

✦

What's missing? The fight. The connection between Dylan and the times he was singing about. Folk music wasn't just a genre — it was a weapon, a movement, a soundtrack to revolution. And yet, A Complete Unknown feels curiously detached from the political and social urgency that fueled his rise.

Maybe that's the point. Maybe the film, like Dylan himself, is dodging expectations, refusing to give a straight answer. Or maybe, in trying so hard to capture the man's contradictions, it accidentally left out the fire that made him matter in the first place.

✦

In the end, A Complete Unknown is as elusive as its subject. Gorgeously crafted, rich in detail, drenched in music — but like Dylan himself, it keeps its cards close to its chest. You might feel like you're watching history happen from a distance, never quite invited inside.

Luckily, sometimes all we need are the vibes.
