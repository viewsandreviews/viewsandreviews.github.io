A Separation / Love / Marriage Story

The Great, Good and Bad of Cinema's Heartbreaks

There's no such thing as selfish love.
Love is about compromise, love is about conciliation.
We cannot love until we embrace the conditions of love.

What is this mismatched medley of films I concatenated, you may ask. The unfortunate truth lies in a moment of sentimental and introspective weakness, which convinced my wandering mind to find questionable parallels between vastly different stories about the discomforts of love. But let me try to justify my need for public lament in this unorganized cinema — and personal, very personal — vivisection.

'Cause today kids, today we're talking about love. Not just any love. We're talking about the kind of love that comes and goes. The love we so desperately crave, the one that leaves us defeated and heartbroken.

Today we'll talk about cinema's many takes on love, heartbreak and our so desperate need for connection. This is a last warning. Things will soon get sappy, depressing, scary, infuriating. But they might also get melancholic, comforting, freeing. We about to fall in love, kids.

✦

Let me start with Marriage Story, probably the most comprehensive and competent of the three in its showing of the progression of fading love, but also the most infuriating one.

Here we follow Charlie and wife Nicole, epitome of obnoxiously perfect couple to an outside viewer. In brief, their story is the ultimate reminder of why performance artists of any kind have a divorce rate of 112%. We follow them from their very last moments of happiness as a couple until the epilogue of their married life.

Director Baumbach does his best at flavouring the whole show with some premium sweet-and-sour sauce of laughs and weeps, and keeps us hanging with the couple until the very end. Unfortunately, us viewers are put through two insurmountable obstacles from the very start: first, we are tasked with the impossible and must suspend our disbelief in seeing Scarlett Johansson married to human meerkat Adam Driver. Second, we are made aware from the very start that this will be not much else than another Hollywoodian ploy in showing how well versed they are at performing auto-fellatio and patting themselves on the back for it during Oscar season.

✦

Gaspar Noé's take on love in his homonymous work is also a partial, force-fed showing of the parallel reality many artists create for themselves and unimaginatively show off. Luckily, his devious mind makes up for this deficit with a more personal, albeit completely maniacal showing.

Unfortunately, due to an obvious lack of post-nut clarity, Noé forgets he is directing a movie for an audience bigger than himself and instead prefers to record his own private fetish anthology. In all seriousness, Love is a two-hour movie, of which one-and-a-half hours are hardcore sex scenes. I've heard there are pornos with more plot development.

For however articulate Noé may have tried to make the one thousand and one nights of coitus in his movie, there's just so much one can tell with a phallus in their mouth — which is also the perfect metaphor to describe his work. Nonetheless, with the patience and devotion of an M. Night Shyamalan fan, my long colonoscopy of his movie did finally manage to find non-fecal matter, but more on that later.

✦

Finally there's A Separation, the one least concerned about dissecting love, but where love is instead enveloped in a tragic blanket of social and geopolitical turmoil. Possibly the least in tune with the love sermon I have been putting you through, but I wanted to re-shine a light on what I still find to be the most humane movie that I have come across this last decade.

Director Farhadi builds from the shoulders of giants of the calibre of Jean Renoir and Yasujiro Ozu to provide a reflection on the human condition, captured during the separation of Nader and Simin. Only glimpses of the past love that may have been are shown here, as their marital troubles intertwine with another troubled couple.

✦

Okay, cool summary Nabin. So, what is this great lesson you so prolixly have been trying to tell us? Well, you may be disappointed to hear it, but since you have made it so far I guess it'd be pointless to give up now, right? It's nothing you may have not realized by yourself already, at least. But sometimes it feels oh so good to say things out loud.

After this improbable trilogy of movies, we learn that love is selfish. We love because we want it; we love because we need it. And we need love just as much as any of our other basic needs. Whether it's out of carnal desires as in Love, out of mutual admiration and sensible attraction as in Marriage Story, or a personal connection stemming from the social requirements that have been nurturing us as in A Separation, we look for love.

Sometimes we even fight for love. Fight against one or many; fight against ourselves. And sometimes we lose in love. "If you fall in love you are a loser," says one of the many sexual partners of Murphy, protagonist of Love. Sometimes love does feel like that; unfair, mismatched. Sometimes love leaves you beat and battered on the floor, just like a '90s coked-up Mike Tyson, minus the medical bills.

✦

But it seems that no matter what, we keep on looking for love. We need to satiate our need for love just as much as we try to satiate our need for 3 AM kebabs, kind of. Only difference is we know how to feed our hungry hungry bellies from the very first instant shining lights hit our eyes. And unfortunately we try to satiate our need for love the same way we satiate our drunken cravings.

For however intelligent us fur-less mammals may have become, we still rely more often than not on trial and error before learning something of value. And for the few fortunates, after many trials, we learn one thing about love.

There's no such thing as selfish love. Love is about compromise, love is about conciliation. We cannot love until we embrace the conditions of love, the struggles of love.

Unfortunately, sometimes it feels like we learn this a little too late. Like Murphy and Electra, Nader and Simin, Charlie and Nicole, it may feel like we understand this only when love is long gone. Only after our stubborn selfishness kept us from loving as much as we need love and took away that dear love. But in truth, that is the same selfishness that keeps us from giving a second shot at love. To love means that sometimes we may have to forgive, to accept; sometimes it means we may have to ask for forgiveness, sometimes we may have to move on.

That sorrowful feeling we are left with after watching the demise of all three of the central relationships of these movies should serve as a reminder of the selflessness needed in love. Only when you stop hurting your baby, your baby won't hurt you no more.

✦

TL;DR — Please go see A Separation. Give Marriage Story a try. Do not see Love with your in-laws around, unless you are hoping for divorce.
