TV Review · Review #50

True Detective

Half the 100, Still Left with Curtis Jackson
Creator Nic Pizzolatto
Year 2014
Starring Matthew McConaughey, Woody Harrelson

Not righteous. Not just.
Not All-American.
True.

Review number 50, after the summer breeze has blown out, and the love of life steeply decays, as leaves in the wind. The middle life crisis of a blog could not be saved by anything less than the apex of small screen entertainment. So here we are, rewinding to a time of wonders, as every time before the now turns into.

The here is a long gone 2014, one to witness the ultimate one-season wonder.

I repeat: one-season wonder.

✦

The time coincided with the apex of the Matthewconnaissaince. The alright x3. Little did Richard Linklater know, that this would be the 21st century's Hollywood motto.

A mostly unknown Nic Pizzolatto embarks on a mission to complete his early — maybe too early — Magnum Opus. Familiar with procedural crime from his first collaborative work, he takes all the knowledge and passion for the genre, and redirects it into an 8-episode masterpiece that revolutionised digital programming forever.

True Detective.

Not righteous. Not just. Not All-American. True. And truly one of a kind series. The type of series that can momentarily suspend the routine of daily living, to suck you into a world of uncertainty, dread and excitement.

✦

That world is a 1995 Louisiana. A world that plays by its own rules. The rules of God almighty. But sometimes the rules of the land are vicious enough to kill a God, just to resurrect another, wicked counterpart.

A few seconds in, we meet our journey companions. Matthew McConaughey is Rustin Cohle — a larger than life homicide detective, and soon to be a lot more. His partner, played by the eternally bald Woody Harrelson, is his counterpart. And to counter is what he does.

The fox and the rabbit. The rebel and the ruler. It is a duo of opposites, not meant to complement each other, if not for screen dynamicity. That is in fact what works best about the pair. Better than any other pair that comes to recent memory.

✦

The first is a man who understands himself, who doesn't like what he understands. The second is a man who's lost in a life most would define as regularly idyllic. The philosophically nihilist Cohle is often confronted by the content, simple Hart. Cohle's outbursts of cynicism — sometimes flares of genius, many others sigma grindset motivational speeches — are flawed in the eyes of men with a purpose to live, something Cohle has been struggling to re-find after personal losses in his life.

As the episodes go on and tension rises, the looney and the donut(less) cop are slowly unfolded to their multiple natures, together with the many, distressing, natural states of men. That is what is explored during their hunt for a serial murderer, whose edge is hidden within the shadowiness of his satanic credo. Through their journey, the pair has to investigate a series of new crimes, and connect them to unsolved mysteries of the past — rekindling the tragedies in their life, testing them on all fronts, helping them find what is missing, and what isn't.

✦

It is a story of oxymorons, both in narration and inspiration. The main questions are uncovered directly, through unexpectedly clever flashforwards. But Pizzolatto keeps you guessing. That's because his True Detective is all about the unanswerable questions. Questions we are often drowned in anxiety by, as the limbo of life is explored between heaven and hell. Questions that force you to look into your inner guts, as the show twists them out with vicious force.

Through the protagonist duo, we learn to be scared of men, while being forced to trust (a minority of) them, as we are only men ourselves. And men is at focus in this gendered masterclass of storytelling, for the worse and for the better.

✦

While it alternates between the opposite leads, our skin crawls in the POV of Hart — a man we understand, as his life has a value in the eyes of others. But in the POV of Rust Cohle, the POV that makes the show still shine ten years later, we stop those feelings. Life turns meaningless if not for the worth of the show itself. Because it's that good of a show.

By the end, we're also too human to let go of them. Their peril must find solace, as we try to find one ourselves. As we flash forward in time, the two protagonists reunite to conclude what was left open, and in the process, to continue to live.

Up to the single last moment, TD helps keep our weak hearts beating, as there is hope in them that it will answer our need for answers. But the answer is in those hearts themselves. The ones that created TD. The ones made for loving TD.

Because for us, in Rust, we trust.
