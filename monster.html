Film Review

Monster

Quiet Devastation, Rashomooned to Perfection
Director Hirokazu Kore-eda
Year 2023
Starring Sakura Ando, Soya Kurokawa, Hinata Hiiragi

Who doesn't love a good slow burner?
The vast majority of you braindead scrollers, for one.
But even you — patron saint of "Can we just skip to the good part?" —
will eventually surrender to the simmering hypnosis
of Kore-eda's filmography.

At first glance, Monster seems like a straightforward drama: a boy named Minato begins to act strangely. His mother, played with soft intensity by Sakura Ando, suspects something is wrong at school. She becomes convinced that one of his teachers is to blame. What unfolds next is one of the many reiterations of Rashomooning — but only one of few who are actually successful at it.

✦

Monster is less a film, more a prism. You look once — you see a child. Look again — a mother. Then a teacher. And in every angle, the monster shifts shape.

Kore-eda slices the narrative into three distinct arcs, each revealing the same events from different perspectives. Not for cheap mystery, but to collapse the very idea of a singular truth. By the end, you're left wondering if the real monster is the person, the institution, or your own assumptions.

✦

At the heart of the story is a fragile but radiant friendship between Minato and another boy, Yori — a connection full of quiet rebellion and coded affection. It's a relationship the adults can't comprehend, and one the system would rather erase than try to understand.

In this way, Monster becomes not just a coming-of-age tale, but a film about queer identity, emotional isolation, and the way children are forced to carry adult shame before they've even figured out who they are.

✦

This is Kore-eda's specialty — taking the domestic and quietly unraveling it to reveal a society beneath it, one held together by shame, silence, and the oppressive weight of tradition. By the time the final segment plays out from the boys' perspective, the film has uncoiled into something soft and bruising.

The violence we assumed, the abuse we were sure of, was never that simple. Kore-eda's genius lies in this refusal to give us closure. What he offers instead is understanding — that empathy must stretch further than we're comfortable with, especially when we think we already know the answer.

✦

Lurking beneath the schoolyard drama is a deep and painful critique of Japanese traditionalism — one that recurs across Kore-eda's body of work. Authority figures protect the system instead of the people within it. Teachers lie to save face.

The monster, as always, is somewhere between the individual and the expectations they're imprisoned by. Shame operates like a silent rulebook. The characters aren't navigating their problems as much as they are being navigated by a society that fears deviation.

✦

Monster is quiet devastation at its best. A film about perspective — not just in narrative structure, but in empathy. You watch it not to solve the mystery, but to dissolve into it.

And if your partner says, "Nothing happened in the first hour," just smile.

They're right. And also completely, gloriously wrong.
