TV Review

Carrie

What's a King to a Carrie
Showrunner Mike Flanagan
Network Prime Video
Year 2026
Episodes 8
Starring Summer H. Howell, Samantha Sloyan, Siena Agudong, Alison Thornton

Human beings in a mob.
What's a mob to a king?
What's a king to a Carrie?
Who can make you bleed from… anywhere.

Please come back, Frank Ocean. I've got a record deal for you. [Unpaid. Exposure only. Much like this blog.]

✦

Mike Flanagan has adapted Stephen King so many times by now that King should probably start charging him rent. This time he takes the one everybody knows — girl, mother, pig's blood, prom, fire — and opens it backwards: 911 calls from a burning school, followed by survivors trying to piece together, documentary-style, how on earth it came to this.

The answer, in 2026, is roughly what you would expect. Carrie White (Summer H. Howell) has been raised in near-total isolation by a devout mother. No phone. No internet. No idea. Then her father dies, she is thrown into public high school, and within a week her worst moment is filmed in a locker room, posted online, and kept alive by an anonymous Instagram account with a name I refuse to type out of respect for the girl.

The pig's blood used to come at the end. Now it arrives with a notification.

✦

At times it is too good of a teen coming-of-age Mean Girls drama — which makes you wonder how Mike Flanagan, a bearded man in his late forties best known for ghosts and sermons, became so good at writing teenage girls.

Luckily for him and his lawyers, there are enough badly written characters to give him an alibi.

The limited cast allows most of the characters to be explored with enough depth not to fall into caricature, for the most part. Alison Thornton's Chris Hargensen is a hiss-worthy queen bee who, annoyingly, comes with a home life that explains her. Siena Agudong's Sue Snell carries a conscience around like a backpack she can't quite take off. And Samantha Sloyan — Flanagan's resident zealot since Midnight Mass — turns Margaret White into something worse than a fanatic: a fanatic with followers.

And then there is Carrie herself, who is the best version of what Eleven could have been from season 4 of Stranger Things onwards. A girl with impossible powers who is still, before anything else, a girl. Eleven bled from the nose. Carrie, as established, can bleed from anywhere.

The monster was never the girl. It was the group chat.

Flanagan fully utilises his eight episodes to build up his monster — and the monster goes beyond any single character. It is the locker room. The principal (Matthew Lillard, ineffectual on purpose, I hope). The parents. The followers. The phones. The town that watched. Human beings in a mob, as Frank would say, if Frank were still saying things.

✦

Which brings us to the strangest choice of the season.

The all-too-reasonable Carrie starts out promptly as a Mary Sue, with a slow slide towards human as she is infected by the sins of her small-town teens. Kind. Curious. Forgiving to a degree that would get her removed from most Oslo housing cooperatives.

What is surprising is Flanagan's decision to let her keep that Sue identity until the very end, while justifying her murderous finale by other — spoilerous — means. I will not tell you what they are. I will only tell you that I spent the final episode waiting for Carrie to become the monster, and the show spent it politely explaining why she didn't have to.

Whether that was a sage decision is up for questioning. Half of me admires the restraint. The other half bought a ticket to watch a girl burn down a prom, and wanted to feel complicit in enjoying it.

What can't be denied is that Carrie remains thoroughly watchable, and rewatchable — and that the ending leaves obvious room for a follow-up season.

One that, for once, I wouldn't be opposed to.

Frank, the offer stands.
