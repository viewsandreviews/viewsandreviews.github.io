Film Review

Heretic

Hugh Grant, Faustian Bargains, and a Freshly Baked Blueberry Pie
Directors Scott Beck & Bryan Woods
Year 2024
Starring Hugh Grant, Sophie Thatcher, Chloe East

Directors Scott Beck and Bryan Woods,
fresh from off-the-track misses,
return to their 2018 good shape —
and make of Hugh Grant a classic thriller villain.

The premise is simple, and one many are familiar with from the distant times of Little Red Riding Hood. After a brief introduction, we meet Sophie Thatcher and Chloe East as Sisters Barnes and Paxton, whose day of door-to-door evangelism takes a dark turn when they knock on Mr. Reed's (Grant) heavy oak entrance.

Mr. Reed shows his first sign of sociopathy when, instead of throwing their pamphlets to the furnace and insulting their intellect — as every gentleman shall do in the face of Mormons — he invites them in with the promise of a freshly baked blueberry pie.

✦

What follows is a tense verbal duel, with Reed spinning theological riddles and existential traps faster than the sisters can flip through their Book of Mormon. He's not just questioning their beliefs — he's taking a sledgehammer to their entire worldview. And yet, like all good horror setups, the sisters don't immediately flee. Maybe it's the pie. Maybe it's the misplaced hope they can convert him. Either way, they stay, and things get weirder.

Grant's portrayal of Reed is a masterclass in playing against type. Gone is the bumbling, floppy-haired charmer; in his place stands a man who could make a reading of the phone book sound like a Faustian bargain.

✦

Beck and Woods take care of the rest, building suspense with every creaky floorboard and flickering candle. Visually, the film is a quiet triumph. The muted tones of Reed's suburban home — lit with natural light that feels a little too cold and sterile — create an oppressive normalcy. It's the kind of place where you half-expect a casserole in the oven and a dead body in the backyard.

Their use of close-ups is especially effective. The subtle tremor of a hand, the twitch of an eyebrow, the barely perceptible crack in a smile — these are the details the camera captures with surgical precision. It's intimate to the point of suffocating, drawing the audience into the uncomfortable closeness of Reed's manipulative charm and the sisters' growing unease. You're not just watching the tension unfold; you're feeling it creep up your spine.

✦

The philosophical debates, while fascinating at first, can get repetitive. By the fourth or fifth monologue about the futility of existence, you might start longing for something — anything — to actually happen. The limited setting also starts to feel, well, too limited. Reed's house is creepy at first, but by the end, it's less "claustrophobic" and more "I wish the space made more of itself."

And then there's the tone. The movie teeters on the edge of being profound but occasionally trips and lands face-first into pretentiousness. Reed's intellectual takedowns range from sharp to college freshman's philosophy paper. These seldom inconsistencies are unfortunately followed by uncharacteristic rebuttals from the Mormon duo, leaving an ultimate sense of displacement.

✦

But do not let the heavy-handed second act ruin the brilliant intentions of Heretic. For a religiously themed movie to be perfect would be heresy by itself.
