Film Review

Weapons

Entertainment First, Message Second, Trauma Forever
Director Zach Cregger
Year 2025
Starring Julia Garner, Josh Brolin, Alden Ehrenreich, Austin Abrams

It is but a twist of faith and irony that allows Horror to confirm itself
as the most alive of genres — alive with originality, alive with chaos,
alive with an army of Millennials now old enough
to handle the reins of new Hollywood.

Now is the turn of Zach Cregger to make his return — after a movie that had no reason to be as good as it was, with a movie that ought to be as good as it is.

What makes Cregger's talent not so different but extremely desirable isn't some lofty arthouse aspiration; it's the simple understanding that movies are entertainment. And that to deliver the best form of entertainment, you best deliver — on all fronts. Whether through comedy, mystery, or a pâté of bodily mutilations. He is always ready to present his full arsenal. His Weapons.

✦

The movie unfolds like a relay race of nightmares, where each character grabs the baton, runs screaming into the abyss, and hands it off to the next unlucky soul — only to finally converge in a sweet and satisfactory finish.

We start in suburbia. Seventeen kids in a small town vanish at 2:17 a.m. like a macabre magic trick. Only one boy, Alex Lilly (Cary Christopher), remains.

From there, the film spins an ensemble nightmare: Julia Garner gives us Justine Gandy, the teacher who might know more than she's letting on; Josh Brolin is Archer Graff, the grieving dad whose life is unraveling at the seams; Alden Ehrenreich plays Paul Morgan, a cop whose moral compass is wobbling; Austin Abrams embodies James, a burnout junkie with too much curiosity; and Amy Madigan appears as Aunt Gladys — creepy and quietly unforgettable.

✦

The plot doesn't just shift perspectives — it mutates. By the time we reach the third thread, the one that drowns itself in paranoia and conspiratorial dread, the movie has fully abandoned genre training wheels. The actor anchoring this arc chews through dialogue like a doomsday prophet at open mic night, selling every frantic theory and bug-eyed breakdown with just enough conviction to make you wonder if they're actually onto something.

Together, these stories fold into each other with that cruel sense of inevitability that only Cregger could cook up. No character is sacred, no performance wasted. Even the most minor roles are played with a level of commitment that makes you double-take. It's because Cregger writes every character like they matter, and the actors seem thrilled to play along.

✦

What's miraculous is how seamless it all feels. One moment you're laughing, the next you're clutching your armrest like it owes you money. The tonal whiplash could break a lesser director's neck, but Cregger treats it like a circus act — balancing on the line between too much and not enough while juggling your expectations like bowling pins.

It helps that the film doesn't take itself too seriously. Sure, there are themes — horror always sneaks in a sermon with its scares — but Cregger isn't here to lecture. He's here to put on a show.

Entertainment first, message second, trauma forever.

✦

In the end, Weapons isn't just another horror flick — it's a loud, unapologetic statement that the genre isn't merely surviving. It's thriving like a cockroach in a fallout shelter.

Cregger's greatest trick might just be convincing us that horror, at its best, is the ultimate variety show: a scream, a laugh, a gasp, and occasionally, an erection.

Point. Set. Match.
