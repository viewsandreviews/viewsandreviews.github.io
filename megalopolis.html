Film Review

Megalopolis

A Golden Tribute to Senile Splendor
Director Francis Ford Coppola
Year 2024
Starring Adam Driver, Giancarlo Esposito, Nathalie Emmanuel

How the mighty have fallen.
Stop.
Hammertime.

Welcome to Megalopolis, ladies, gentlemen and +. Here you may enjoy exploring claims of a deeper understanding of life, without ever actually having to watch Coppola's not-so-figurative Apocalypse Now, as no witnesses will ever be found.

Megalopolis is the high-rise tower built on the shoulders of an auteur giant who now resembles an uncle whose wisdom has grown "unique" with age.

✦

First, let's address the elephant in the gold-dusted donut shop: Francis Ford Coppola, at the ripe age of "we really should have an age limit for this," steps out of a decade-long hiatus. But rather than descending from the mountaintop with clarity and vision, he arrives with a suitcase full of confusing, jumbled allegories — like someone who accidentally grabbed the metaphors from his "outtakes" pile. His latest offering is a Metropolis, but not in the way of Fritz Lang. No, this Metropolis is an eco-friendly composite town that's not so much futuristic as it is a fever dream of someone who just woke up from a 20-year nap.

It's evident from the first golden beam of skyscraper sun that Megalopolis is trying to be deep. This is the type of film that's so sure of its brilliance, it probably wears sunglasses indoors. Coppola fills his scenes with grand gestures and heavy-handed symbolism, offering us more "meaning" than even the poor actors know what to do with. But when every moment is meant to be meaningful, you end up with a syncretic mess where everything is vaguely important, but nothing is particularly interesting.

✦

Back in his glory days, Coppola's allegories worked because they were rooted in gritty, real worlds and with characters we could actually care about. The Godfather gave us loyalty and corruption wrapped up in a family saga we felt invested in — Michael wasn't just a symbol, he was a guy trying (and failing) to keep it together. Apocalypse Now didn't need to shout "SYMBOLISM!" every two seconds; it simply dropped Willard in the jungle, and the madness unfolded naturally. But Megalopolis? Here, allegories float around like helium balloons with no one to hold the strings, leaving us in a glittering but empty landscape. It's as if Coppola took the nuanced themes we once admired and smacked them with a "deep thoughts" label, hoping that would be enough.

Coppola's semi-senile hand manages to turn titans of modern and past cinema into embellished props of The Room. Rumours of excessive use of improv turn out to be all too real, with many of the greats improvising themselves as kindergarten play stars. As the old saying goes, "A good director makes a good actor," and unfortunately, the reverse seems all too true here. Even the titans can't save a sinking New Atlantis.

✦

Then there's the question of Coppola's true objective with Megalopolis. Is it to craft a dystopian warning? A critique of modern society? A meditation on the collapse of urban life? Whatever he set out to do, what he's actually done is create a meta-experiment on the pitfalls of ambition. This is Heaven's Gate all over again — the only "Gate" being the one guarding Hollywood's budget office, locked tightly behind him.

If Megalopolis was meant to be a showcase of cinematic virtuosity, it's instead a monument to self-indulgence, a tower of Babel where language and reason fail, and the view from the top reveals only a reflection of one's own vanity. At its core, this film might indeed be an allegory for something — though I suspect it's simply a reminder that sometimes, even the greatest of giants can stumble spectacularly.
