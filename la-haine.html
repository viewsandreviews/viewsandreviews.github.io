ChatGPT ain't got shit on me.

A butterfly whose flapping effect is loved by cinema as much as it is hated by French lawmakers. The marginalized of the past are still the victims of the future, and their story is here told in black and white by Mathieu Kassovitz.

✦

It feels appropriate that for my 30th review, I describe the archetypical movie which brought me to love cinema, only to find me in an existential self-reflection. A reflection focused on my growth, and my internalized battle to understand whether this growth has brought me more distance to cinema, or if the French simply ain't got what it takes.

Yes, I indeed come with a hot take. La Haine ain't it. It ain't Do the Right Thing. It ain't Trainspotting. It definitely ain't City of God.

✦

But let me tell you what it is. It still is a great telling of social disparity, through the eyes of a trio of friends, whose colored personalities and skins transcend the monochromatic film. Vinz, Saïd, and Hubert. Three delinquents from the banlieues of Paris, as my French ex would describe them. Their 24 hours following one of the many riots that have seen many of the immigrant youngsters of the time against the police, their next 24 hours are fleshed out in an hour and a half, giving a glimpse into their whole being. Their struggles.

All of the rawness. All the rage. All of it, characteristic of that new realism so popular once ago. All of it is definitely there, carried by the performances of Vincent Cassel and Saïd Taghmaoui. Everything feels authentic. To a fault.

✦

The fault of time, which strips the merit of authenticity and novelty. See, a movie like La Haine simply can't survive time. The hate of the past is still the hate of the future. But this hate has been probed so much that it is now history. And history is not enough anymore, at least on the big screen.

Self-reflection is what is then left. And if there's anything I hate more than reflecting, it is re-reflecting.

Who's a good boy? You're a good boy!
