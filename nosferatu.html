Film Review

Nosferatu

Children of the Night, What Movies They Make
Director Robert Eggers
Year 2024
Starring Bill Skarsgård, Lily-Rose Depp, Nicholas Hoult

Sometimes, to (re)-set the standard,
you gotta break some Eggers.

After a string of more than successful folklore gems, Eggers resurrects an old project — as old as cinema itself. One that he postponed many years ahead, as fears of egomaniacal takeovers pushed an obvious dream further in time. And to finally turn these fears into nightmares, fueled by a mastered craft of filmmaking and tone-setting.

His Nosferatu isn't so much a remake as it is a séance. Eggers didn't just summon the spirit of Murnau's 1922 classic — he set the table, lit some black candles, and sacrificed a goat to ensure this version is dripping in moody grandeur and unhinged energy.

✦

His reverence for the classic film may be mistaken for laziness, as little is added to the original in terms of plot. Simpleton husband, in search of riches, travels to a place beyond time and civilization to sign a contract with a frightful count. What was supposed to be a lucrative real estate affair for the newlywed soon reveals consequences worse than the 2008 housing crash.

Nicholas Hoult's Thomas Hutter makes for the perfect husband in distress, albeit with a peculiarly British-heavy tone that seems to afflict the majority of an otherwise vibrantly German Wisburg. Lily-Rose Depp, meanwhile, steps up to the world of acclaimed cinema — the role of distressed yet empowered heroine filling a previously flimsy career.

✦

Bill Skarsgård's Count Orlok is both unsettling and alive. His every movement — inspired by the classic's silhouette-esque style — is unpredictable and hypnotic. But here's the kicker: Orlok isn't just a monster.

He is not hidden from us, appearing only on cue for a fright. He is instead shown — shadowed by tricks of light, the sun withheld from full revelation. But he is ultimately visible, physically as much as he is revealed for his character. He is as much an invincible force of nature as he is a man in love.

His relationship with Depp's Ellen — significantly expanded from the original story — is one of a cursed affair. Destinies intertwined until the end. As tragic in its conclusion as it is in execution.

✦

The lore and world that was so successfully built up starts, at a point, to show small yet noticeable crevices. It lacks a final note of mysticism — one that would have helped the film create a new mythos, to place it in the halls of fame shared by the original or modern masterpieces like Hereditary.

But while mythos may not have been created, it was for sure reinvigorated — with dignity and humbleness. Eggers makes you feel every ounce of the characters' despair. The director doesn't just direct films; he crafts nightmares.

✦

The cinematography is jaw-dropping, every frame more akin to a cursed oil painting than simple camera work. His world of lights and shadows is hypnotic — not just thanks to mastered craft, but through a deepened understanding of fear and desire. The fog feels alive, and the castles so damp you can practically smell the mildew.

Eggers' Nosferatu is ultimately a new standard of horror cinema. One that requires scares to be as suspenseful as they are mesmerizing. One that will likely inspire many others to bring a whole new set of decrepit life to the genre.
