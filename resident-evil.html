The best Resident Evil film is barely a Resident Evil film — and that is precisely the point.
Film Review

Resident Evil

The Eviler the Resident, the Better the Movie
Director Zach Cregger
Year 2026
Runtime 93 min
Starring Austin Abrams, Paul Walter Hauser, Zach Cherry

How do you make the best Resident Evil movie? You forget about what Resident Evil is even about.

To say that would be a disservice to what Zach Cregger actually accomplishes here — because the man hasn't forgotten about Resident Evil so much as he's decided to approach it in possibly the only way that it should have ever been approached.

What Cregger does is stop answering "how do we translate the game into a film," for the displeasure of redditors all around, and instead ask himself something far more simple and interesting: "what kind of film would I want to make if I happened to live in this universe?"

The answer, it turns out, is a very good one.

✦

For those who missed the memo: Cregger has never seen a single Resident Evil film. And that is the type of unburdened approach that could turn a franchise with critic scores equivalent to DJT's SAT scores into my blood sugar level scores. What he hasn't missed, is the games. Most presumably the new, improved ambiance series. And that's what he captures in the Prague streets of a Czech production budget doing excellent work as Raccoon City, and presumably many sleepless nights with a controller.

The result is a film set not around Capcom's love child Leon Kennedy, but around Bryan, a medical courier who has the singular misfortune of attempting a delivery to Raccoon City General Hospital on the one night the city decides to end. Austin Abrams has full-on spotlight for the whole show, and doesn't let the opportunity go afloat. He brings the full premium package of regular guy in apocalypse nightmare — missed shots, goofy stumbles, genuine fright — but most of all what was missing from both several of the games and the majority of the previous films: humour. Good humour.

The dry, character-driven, earned kind — not the winking self-awareness that tends to curdle franchise horror into parody, but the kind that emerges organically when people behave like actual people in impossible situations. And the combo of Cregger and Abrams build the almost perfect balance of aware horror comedy, without renouncing too often to exciting scares.

✦

The cinematography — courtesy of Dariusz Wolski, who has no business being this good at making Prague look like the end of the world — is the kind that game fans will clock immediately. The camera angles, the lighting choices, the specific geometry of dread when something is lurking just outside the frame: Cregger has done his homework, and for fans of the source material these moments land with the particular satisfaction of an Easter egg that's actually been hidden somewhere worth looking, without inconveniencing too often the newcomers.

That is expectedly also where the film's limitations live. Because it still has to be a Resident Evil film. The lore demands its tribute. The franchise requires its gestures. And in moments where Cregger has to service the IP rather than his own instincts, you can feel the creative leash tighten — not enough to trip the film, but enough to remind you that somewhere a producer has a checklist. These constraints keep Resident Evil one step behind the best horror of the year, which in 2026 remains Obsession — a film that had no IP to honour, no checklist to clear, just a stick and a wish and its own creator's unburdened imagination running at full speed.

✦

But here's the thing about constraints: in the right hands, they become their own kind of creativity. And for fans who have spent years watching the franchise squander its own potential, watching Cregger honour the games while making something genuinely his own is not a compromise.

It's a love letter.

And unlike most love letters, this one doesn't embarrass itself.
