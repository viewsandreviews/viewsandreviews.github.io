The best Resident Evil film is barely a Resident Evil film — and that is precisely the point.
Film Review

Resident Evil

The Best RE Movie Is Not Really a RE Movie
Director Zach Cregger
Year 2026
Runtime 93 min
Starring Austin Abrams, Paul Walter Hauser, Zach Cherry

How do you make the best Resident Evil movie? You forget about what Resident Evil is even about.

To say that would be a disservice to what Zach Cregger actually accomplishes here — because the man hasn't forgotten about Resident Evil so much as he's decided, with considerable nerve, to approach it sideways. Where every previous attempt at adapting Capcom's beloved franchise asked "how do we translate the game into a film," Cregger asked something more interesting: "what kind of film would I want to make if I happened to live in this universe?"

The answer, it turns out, is a very good one.

For those who missed the memo: Cregger has never seen a single Resident Evil film. He said so himself, openly, without apparent embarrassment. This is either an act of directorial hubris or directorial wisdom, and the finished product lands firmly on the side of the latter. Unburdened by the franchise's cinematic history — which, let's be honest, is less a legacy than a cautionary tale — he went straight to the source. The games. Specifically RE2, RE3, the Prague streets of a Czech production budget doing excellent work as Raccoon City, and presumably many sleepless nights with a controller and a very specific kind of determination.

✦

The result is a film set not around Leon Kennedy or Claire Redfield or any of the characters whose stories you already know, but around Bryan, a medical courier who has the singular misfortune of attempting a delivery to Raccoon City General Hospital on the one night the city decides to end. Austin Abrams plays Bryan with the particular energy of someone who has been thrown into a situation so far beyond his professional remit that there is almost something comedic about his persistence. Almost, because Cregger knows exactly when to let you laugh and exactly when to take that laugh away.

Which brings us to what Cregger injects into the RE franchise that was missing from both several of the games and the majority of the previous films: humour. Good humour. The dry, character-driven, earned kind — not the winking self-awareness that tends to curdle franchise horror into parody, but the kind that emerges organically when people behave like actual people in impossible situations. Zach Cherry, as Dave the Umbrella scientist, is doing work here that belongs in a much better regarded film than this franchise has historically produced. Paul Walter Hauser, as Carl, proves once again that he is incapable of giving a bad performance even when the material asks him to be broadly, enjoyably ridiculous.

✦

The cinematography — courtesy of Dariusz Wolski, who has no business being this good at making Prague look like the end of the world — is the kind that game fans will clock immediately. The camera angles, the lighting choices, the specific geometry of dread when something is lurking just outside the frame: Cregger has done his homework, and for fans of the source material these moments land with the particular satisfaction of an Easter egg that's actually been hidden somewhere worth looking.

That is also, eventually, where the film's limitations live.

Because it still has to be a Resident Evil film. The lore demands its tribute. The franchise requires its gestures. And in moments where Cregger has to service the IP rather than his own instincts, you can feel the creative leash tighten — not enough to trip the film, but enough to remind you that somewhere a producer has a checklist. These constraints keep Resident Evil one step behind the best horror of the year, which in 2026 remains Obsession — a film that had no IP to honour, no checklist to clear, just a stick and a wish and Cregger's own unburdened imagination running at full speed.

✦

But here's the thing about constraints: in the right hands, they become their own kind of creativity. And for fans who have spent years watching the franchise squander its own potential, watching Cregger honour the games while making something genuinely his own is not a compromise.

It's a love letter.

And unlike most love letters, this one doesn't embarrass itself.
