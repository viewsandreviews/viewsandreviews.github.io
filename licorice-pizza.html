Film Review

Licorice Pizza

An Unlikely Recipe for Nonconformist Love
Director Paul Thomas Anderson
Year 2021
Starring Alana Haim, Cooper Hoffman, Sean Penn

Just like licorice, this will undoubtedly make for a flavour
that one will adjust and learn to enjoy over time.

While my Italianness had initially made me hesitate from watching a movie with such a blasphemous title, I could not resist the temptation of uncovering this new-age classic from one of the best to ever do it. And Licorice Pizza, by the marvelous Mr. Paul Thomas Anderson, is likely to be a premature classic, continuing the director's successful string of showpieces — although with a caress more alike that of his more gentle colleague and coming-of-age collector, Richard Linklater.

PTA backs into his beloved '70s, with a more nostalgic and less aroused eye than in Boogie Nights, to narrate the sweet tale of Alana Kane (Alana Haim) and her 15-year-old soulmate, Gary Valentine (Cooper Hoffman). The late Philip Seymour Hoffman's offspring delivers a sober, star-making performance, much alike his co-protagonist, alone for the sobriety part.

✦

Gary is a child star. A go-getter, with no qualm in adventuring into new territories, never discouraged by his at times questionable financial endeavors. If the movie were set in modern times, Gary would undoubtedly be convincing all his classmates on some savvy crypto investment.

Such is the confidence, determination and slight naivety of Mr. Valentine, that asking 10-years-older Alana comes to him as natural and comfortable as a Catholic priest at a pediatric hospital. And if you find this profane humour inappropriate, you won't enjoy what PTA has to narrate about their concerning, improbable love.

✦

Alana is more than aware of the siren-starting age gap between her and Gary. Unlike her optimistic, never-surrendering suitor, Alana is much concerned about her lack of decade-appropriate accomplishments. No soon-to-be marital partner, no decisive, braggable career path — that is all a big no-no from her Ashkenazi progenitors, and she's here to forcefully take action to make something out of her decaying ovaries, according to the medical convictions of the time.

The two move around one of those valleys often protagonist of American teenage cinema. Sometimes with rushing careens, other times with relaxed saunters — well-nigh never stopping to catch a breath, if not for that one moment. That moment when both realize and both accept, after their many amorous encounters and glimpses of alternating jealousy, that their love is inescapable to them.

✦

A love joyfully matured throughout various encounters with other giddy characters, which often are more caricatures than personas. PTA's rest of the pizza world is filled with Simpsons-esque adults — which may be either his past vision of the American Graffiti world he grew up in, or what he'd expect the protagonist duo to see them as. Fact remains that this adds a necessary silliness as well as an overplayed satire against the commonly scapegoated Hollywood movie industry.

On the downside, this break from the real adds some distracting or forgetful moments to the much-ado-about-nothing tale. And unfortunately Anderson's lingering about past emotions and experiences, for however whimsical and personal it may feel, ends up lacking an engaging bite-back at times.

So even though, for the time being, I would have to compare this PTA pizza recipe unfavorably to the bestest of his previous works, just like licorice, this will undoubtedly make for a flavour that one will adjust and learn to enjoy over time.
