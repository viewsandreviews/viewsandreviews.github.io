Film Review

Rebel Ridge

A Two-Hour Tension Drive That Hums at Shining-Level Intensity
Director Jeremy Saulnier
Year 2024
Starring Aaron Pierre

You either die a hero, or live long enough
to become a direct-to-Netflix movie director.
Luckily for Jeremy Saulnier, the streaming fate is better
than many of its alternatives in this day and age.
Luckily for us, Jeremy Saulnier is still Jeremy fucking Saulnier.

The man who once made audiences wince at the sheer force of his on-screen carnage — with modern cult classics Green Room and Blue Ruin — now seems more interested in testing patience and his surreal thriller-making talents. Rebel Ridge is a two-hour tension drive that hums at Shining-level intensity: unbroken, unrelenting, and in its restraint, both admirable and slightly maddening.

This is still unmistakably a Saulnier film: the precise framing, the sharp edges to every line of dialogue, the world populated by people who look like they've already lived three other movies before we meet them. The director cements his proclivity to make of violence a story of systemic oppression, always lurking within the lowly streets of mid-America.

What he does differently is premise and execution.

✦

The premise itself — half action-thriller, half civics lesson — might be too silly and Netflix-y to support the weight of the ideas Saulnier piles on top. Yet he commits so completely that it almost works in spite of itself. You keep expecting the film to collapse under the tension between its pulp roots and its smarter-than-it-should-be ambitions, but instead it just… keeps going, fueled by a soundtrack that feels like a meth head's heartbeat: jittery and unstoppable.

✦

And that's merit to the execution. In a rebellious and contrarian shift, Saulnier makes a statement: You know I can do this. I just don't need to anymore.

What was once known as build-up now becomes judge, jury, and executioner itself. The expectations of a big, explosive action sequence are thwarted at every corner — to the point where one forgets about masculine pleasing via gratuitous violence, and fully embraces Saulnier's procedural and surgical breakdown of man vs. power.

✦

His main star, Aaron Pierre, supports this mission as the Rambo-esque drifter protagonist — a man of few words but all the presence in the world. He carries the role with that dangerous kind of charisma: equal parts menace and magnetism, making it impossible to look away. Every scene he's in feels alive, even when he's doing nothing more than sizing up the room.

Pierre makes the film's moral center tangible without ever turning preachy — which is no small feat considering the script occasionally flirts with soapbox territory.

✦

Whether Saulnier intended Rebel Ridge as a statement on justice, corruption, and survival, or simply as an exercise in pure cinematic craft, he ends up doing both. The film works as a tense, stripped-down anti-action piece, but it also lingers afterward — leaving you with questions about power, morality, and what we're willing to do when the world refuses to play fair.

It may not be the movie his earlier fans expected, but in its stubborn, almost spiteful refusal to play the obvious notes, Rebel Ridge cements Saulnier as a filmmaker who'd rather challenge his audience than indulge them.

And frankly, in an age where so many action films feel like they were designed by focus group, that's worth celebrating — no matter how much you might wish he'd just light up a meaty redneck-flavored meat fest.
