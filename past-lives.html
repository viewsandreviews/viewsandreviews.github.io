Film Review

Past Lives

A Clock May Be Rewound
Director Celine Song
Year 2023
Starring Greta Lee, Teo Yoo, John Magaro

A clock may be rewound,
but time will not be forgotten.
— Christopher Lloyd

If one were to tell me my top picks for best movie would have consecutively been dominated by the Far Eastern brothers and sisters, I guess I would have found that quite reasonable. Fresh in memory for their flashy dances and torture-like pop screeching, their not-so-hidden talents behind the screen have been ripening since the early days of Bong Joon-ho thrills. But it's not only thrills that they offer nowadays — they rapidly specialise in the art of being human.

This has been my 2023 highlight, and brings me to celebrate the latest gem excavated by A24 studios. A gem by the name of Past Lives — fruit of no forced labour, but creative introspection. That is what a fresh-faced Celine Song proposes to us, as she brings closure to a reflection of her past and present. A reflection that may shadow a few of us, me included, but that'll still gently approach the furthest.

✦

Here we start, with a small snippet of the present from a curious observer's point of view. That's where we meet Nora, our middle-aged protagonist, her husband, and her past. One hunk of a past, dare I say. But before things get awkward, we are transported to Nora's native land, the second-best Korea. Transported in time as well, as the now twelve-year-old Nora is no more-a.

She's back at being Na Young — a sometimes bright, always proud teenager, cherishing her last moments in the motherland. Nora is one of the many youngsters of the then-developing nation to look for sweeter syrups in the land of the Moose. Soon to be immigrating to Canada, Nora is more than ready to say goodbye to her best friend and early love, Hae Sung — although a goodbye never comes. That missed goodbye will soon be at focus, as readiness can only float away with time.

✦

We slowly grow with Nora, alternating between spans of twelve years. We see her evolve and chase new dreams, always with the determination that Hae Sung saw in her in childhood. And it's in the midst of all of this that a chance to realign fate comes, sponsored by Mark Zuckerberg. The two reconnect online. They share their lives, more perpendicular than ever.

But that does not last long. Nora retreats from their video exchanges — she wants to focus on her career as a writer. Her present. It's in this present that she meets her future husband Arthur, a fellow writer and early patient of the sunflower-coloured fever. A present that she's content with. A present she learned to love, or maybe accept. Until an opportunity to explore a life that has never been is given to her.

✦

First-time director Celine Song invites us to relive her memories, and in doing so rows us into our own sea of nostalgia. Her story reveals the modern outlook of traditions — fresh minds, moulded by the heritage of the elders. Unglamorous traditions, as they embrace the struggles of immigration as much as of identity. Everything is modest, simple, and authentic. Organic people, lost in mundane urban landscapes, tinted in shades of emotion. The colour palette is reduced to the bare necessary, and makes of necessity a virtue.

But simplicity doesn't negate complexity. Her characters think, often out loud — more so than us regular folks. They share their fears, let them run through their partners, forcing us to question whether we have been doing it wrong all along.

✦

Each scene slowly pulses onto the next one, exhaling right when it's necessary — lingering on the screen until lonesome memories are brought alive. And after a long journey, we're happy to know that it's not over. There is still time. There are still lives to live. In present and in past.
