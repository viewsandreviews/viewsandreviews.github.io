Film Review

Dune

2021's Blockbuster Scene Just Got *removes sunglasses* Spicier
Director Denis Villeneuve
Year 2021
Starring Timothée Chalamet, Rebecca Ferguson, Oscar Isaac, Javier Bardem

Fasten your spaceship seat-belts (or your sand-worm saddles).
There's a new neurotic fandom in town.

Fasten your spaceship seat-belts (or your sand-worm saddles). There's a new neurotic fandom in town.

With 2021's Dune, Villeneuve continues his mission to usurp Nolan's throne as the go-to thinking man blockbuster machine. Not only that, but he proves nay-sayers wrong by realizing the impossible and making what even critic-favourite David Lynch epically failed in doing. A cohesive, modestly profound and fan-fulfilling Dune experience; that is the result of a five year-long production and decades long development malebolgia.

His new and improved Dune not only satisfies all but the most purist buffs of Frank Herbert's novel, but succeeds in enticing newer, attention-deficient audiences of any shade, background and sexual orientation.

✦

Villeneuve's amour for the original material, combined with his newly found expertise in commanding sci-fi epics are by themselves much of the reason for the undeniable success of his latest feature. But even his nigh omnipotence in epic film direction are not enough to defeat the villainous demons of modern Hollywood blockbusters.

Dune's protagonist, gloomy heartthrob Timothée Chalamet (sorry Timmy, got frustrated after re-spelling your name for the 4th time) does an adequate job at interpreting the space-desert-odyssey hero. His sparkles of expressiveness are sporadic, but timed efficiently enough to keep us on a moderately firm grip during the typical (for the time being) hero's journey shenanigans.

Not that I would fault him for his emotionally-impaired performance; he already showed us his incredible chops by convincingly me-tooing a peach in Call Me By Your Name.

✦

Chalamet and all his Cosmopolitan cover-of-the-year co-stars seem to be tasked to avoid overshadowing the intrigues and the world-building slowly — like, really f*ckin' slowly — unfolding around them. Sole exception goes for the aggressively bombastic Stilgar, leader of Fremen, played by aggressively attractive Javier Bardem. Please pardon my brain-dead humour.

Tis but a shame, seeing how brilliantly and tastefully Villeneuve was able to capture human laments and the torments of frenzied vengeance in his early works (see Incendies and Prisoners). Nevertheless, while many of the characters are turned into inanimate objects, Villeneuve still revels in recreating this dystopian world and proudly lingers on its solemn landscapes, at times with the self-indulgence of a sadistic anti-vaxxer.

The geopolitical conflicts and key factions are explored well-enough to let us draw the obvious parallels with past and present history, without having to rely on Dora the Explorer levels of exposition.

✦

Talking about exposition, it is undeniable that the first third of the movie suffers from the unavoidable infographics necessary to introduce new audiences to Herbert's homonymous world. One of the simplest solutions my infallible mind would have explored to alleviate its painful dullness would have been to summarize the whole shebang into a Lord of the Rings-styled narration.

All this being said, I'll be more than happy if the upcoming epilogue is as equally qualified as the first half. Now, if you'll excuse me, my sand-worm got some dunes to ride, if ya catch my drift.
