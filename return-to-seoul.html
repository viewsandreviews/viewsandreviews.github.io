Film Review

Return to Seoul

A Croissant-filled Dumpling
Director Davy Chou
Year 2022
Starring Park Ji-min

What distinguishes a great slow burn movie from a slowly burning borefest?
The viewer.

That is the unfortunate truth that keeps the genre from being the go-to of your average Friday night cinema outing. And yet, paradoxically, it is the same truth that makes it the undisputed darling of every festival jury.

The qualities that make general audiences shift uncomfortably in their seats — the silence, the refusal to explain, the trust that you'll keep up — are precisely the qualities that make critics write things like "a haunting meditation on selfhood" at one in the morning in a hotel room in the south of France.

Return to Seoul is absolutely that kind of film. Whether that's a compliment depends entirely on you.

✦

Davy Chou's film follows Freddie, a 25-year-old French woman of Korean origin who lands in Seoul on what begins as an impulsive detour — her original holiday plans fell through, she ended up in Korea instead, as one does — and finds herself, with increasing reluctance, pulled toward the birth parents who gave her up for adoption.

Now. Freddie.

If you put a dumpling in a chest full of croissants, it will inevitably ask itself why it tastes so different from the rest. The trouble with Freddie is that she'd also ask that in a chest full of dumplings.

She is a woman so thoroughly unmoored from any fixed identity that she approaches both France and Korea with the same armed detachment — as if belonging somewhere, or by someone, might be used against her. She is as true to herself as she is unlikeable, which is a combination that takes real filmmaking nerve to sustain for two hours without either softening her or punishing her for it.

Chou does neither. He simply watches.

✦

But it is mainly thanks to Park Ji-min, in her debut performance, that Chou is allowed to watch without much added creative fatigue. Because this slow-burn is constantly enflamed by Ji-min's performance. More than a performance, almost a reality.

She is magnetic in the way that difficult people are magnetic — you cannot look away, even when, especially when, you want to. The performance carries the film through its quietest passages on sheer force of characterisation alone.

✦

What Return to Seoul understands, and what separates it from lesser films in the same register, is that the search for identity is rarely a journey toward something.

It is usually a series of collisions with things that were supposed to answer the question and didn't. Freddie meets her birth father. She meets, eventually, her birth mother. Neither encounter delivers what the genre usually promises. There is no catharsis neatly packaged in a tearful reunion. There is just the complicated, ongoing fact of being a person who came from somewhere she doesn't recognise, raised somewhere she doesn't fully belong.

A broken glass that keeps on cracking.

✦

Where the film stumbles is not in content but in quantity. Return to Seoul spans nearly a decade of Freddie's life, and in doing so begins to crowd itself with events, relationships, and time jumps that push well beyond the natural limits of what a single feature film should reasonably contain.

What begins as a tightly wound character study gradually loosens at the seams — not catastrophically, but noticeably. Like a haircut that was neatly prepped up in the morning, only to unravel the unbeatable signs of hairloss by end of day. I too am going through internal crises, Freddie.

By the third act you begin to feel the weight of all that accumulated time, and not always in the way the film intends. It is a film that would rather show you everything about Freddie than trust you to infer it. Which is a strange instinct for a film so otherwise committed to restraint.

✦

And yet.

Return to Seoul lingers. Freddie's melancholy lingers. Its soundtrack lingers — looping a masterpiece from JungHwa Lee, as haunting as anything Jim Morrison could ever come up with.

It's an uneasiness that remains hard to wash off; a suffering too heavy to not share. It may not be at its immediate conclusion that we start to feel that load, but we will certainly feel it whenever our windows are drumming the beats of mild rain showers. Or when that mirror reflection of ourselves starts to eerily remind us of our aging parents.

A glass breaking, as if it was meant to be broken.

F you, Freddie.
