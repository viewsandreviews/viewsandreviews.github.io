Film Review

Civil War

The Terror of Prophetic Dystopia
Director Alex Garland
Year 2024
Starring Kirsten Dunst, Wagner Moura, Cailee Spaeny

An apocalypse where what is rotten
is not flesh but empathy.
Suck on these, Shakespeare.

Welcome back, talented mister Garland — and goodbye mediocre Garland. After a mishap that could have easily torpedoed into a spiral of irrelevance, à la Nicolas Winding Refn, the floral man is back to his usual shape. The message here is to enjoy the fruits the world has gifted you before it's too late. Or something like that.

He is also the reason I got to experience my first 4DX semi-torture, so thanks for that. The man is indeed back at doing what he is known for — poignant political commentary blended with esoteric still shots. Kind of like that lonesome neighbour professor who has a telescope in his attic pointed at your window.

✦

The film portrays a modern dystopia — or, more accurately, an apocalypse where what is rotten is not flesh but empathy. Acclaimed war photographer and journalist Lee Smith (Kirsten Dunst) is seen at yet another rally in an ultra-divided USA. The recession has already happened, as the southern area has removed its stars from the once proud American Union. The movie tries to capture the essence of wrath that has characterised the modern American political landscape, yet comes a little late to coincide with the boiling point, reached a few years from now.

Smith's journey is one of high risk, as she will have to traverse the urban battlefields of a fractured nation with the mission of interviewing a Trumpesque president before his inevitable fall. She is followed by fellow photographers and friends Sammie and Joel — closest things to what friends used to be defined as.

✦

A young photographer by the name of Jessie Cullen is picked up along the way. Jessie is still naive to the terrors that will be captured by her lenses, photographic and non. But Smith takes a chance on the younglin — to mentor her in the ways of an apathetic messenger, hoping that the messages will reinvigorate the lost empathy into the world.

In what is possibly a self-reflection of his ever-more preaching testaments, Garland questions the morality of a profession that is seen with growing distrust. The quartet of journalists at focus is a random one, but Garland makes of this randomness a message of duty. As society falls, people rise up to their missions — ones that they set for themselves to make something out of an existence where motives are funnelled to the bare minimum.

✦

Kirsten Dunst continues in her rocketing trajectory of critical acclaim, with a stoic performance that leaves much space for human reconnection. Her companions do not trail far behind, as Garland keeps the focus on their exposure to the events more than on a world that has little fantasy instilled in it.

Civil War, for all of its merits, still trails distantly from the peaks set by Garland's debut in Ex Machina, with the brilliant performances making up for questionable soundtrack decisions and jarring transitions. But Garland almost takes a step back — with much-needed humility — to allow viewers time to reflect and reconnect fiction to fact. The final question, often captured with ultra-noir satire, focuses on the absurdity of invisible division.

But ultimately, true division was only experienced by my asscheeks, shaking more than a recently divorced 39-year-old mum of two at a spin class, owing to a 4DX seat like no others.
