TV Review

Widow's Bay

The Most British-Feeling American Series of the Decade
Creator Katie Dippold
Director Hiro Murai
Network Apple TV+
Starring Matthew Rhys, Kate O'Flynn

"I just want to say one word to you. Just one word. Are you listening?
Timing." — 9/11 Hijackers.

That's it. That's the whole secret.

Every comedian who ever made you spit your drink, every horror director who ever made you sleep with the lights on — they all got there the same way. Not the joke, not the scare. The pause before it.

Katie Dippold gets this. Hiro Murai gets this. And together, in a misty little New England town with an all too suspiciously high rating on TripAdvisor, they've made the single most British-feeling American series of the decade.

✦

Widow's Bay follows Tom Loftis, the skeptical mayor of a town whose residents are absolutely, unshakeably convinced that their home is cursed. Tom does not believe them. Tom, played by Matthew Rhys, would very much like everyone to calm down and focus on the tourism initiative he has been working very hard on.

What Dippold has quietly pulled off here is something more interesting than it first appears. On the surface, Widow's Bay presents itself as a horror series — misty coastline, ominous locals, things that probably shouldn't happen, happening. And the scares are genuinely there.

But the real catch, once Dippold has you on the hook, is the comedy. Specifically, the kind of dry, patient, character-driven comedy that Americans historically outsource to the British and then act surprised when it works.

There's a reason this feels like a BBC production that somehow ended up on Apple TV+. It's not the accents. It's the restraint. The willingness to let a joke breathe, to let the absurdity of a situation land without underlining it three times in red pen.

It's the haute couture version of what the Scary Movie franchise was trying to do in 2000 — except instead of spoofing horror, Widow's Bay genuinely is horror, and the comedy emerges organically from the collision between the supernatural and the relentlessly mundane. Between a town full of true believers and one very tired mayor who just wants a positive write-up in the New York Times.

✦

Matthew Rhys is, quietly, doing some of his best work here. Tom Loftis is not a buffoon. He is not the straight man in a farce. He is a fundamentally decent, fundamentally out-of-his-depth man watching his professional ambitions dissolve in real time, and Rhys plays every stage of that dissolution with the kind of specificity that makes you feel genuinely bad for someone who is also, objectively, quite funny to watch suffer.

Kate O'Flynn's Patricia Moyer is, in many ways, the series' secret weapon. Where Tom Loftis anchors the show in weary rationalism, Patricia operates on an entirely different frequency. She is, crucially, a fully realised person before she is a punchline — which puts her several rungs above what this type of character usually gets away with. Until it doesn't.

✦

If there's a but — and there is — it comes in moments where the comedy loses its nerve. Where the subtlety that makes the show sing gives way to broader, louder gags that feel imported from a different, lesser series. The genius of Widow's Bay at its best is that it doesn't announce its jokes. When it starts announcing them, you notice. And once you notice, the timing — that one word, the whole secret — slips just slightly out of frame.

The pacing occasionally follows suit. Some episodes find a rhythm so comfortable and propulsive you barely feel the 40 minutes pass. Others meander, as if the writers have correctly identified that the town contains more interesting threads than they have episodes to pull them. Several storylines feel like they're building toward something that the season ultimately doesn't quite get to.

Whether that's a deliberate season two setup or a first season finding its feet, it's hard to say. What's clear is that the show has more world than it's currently using — which is either a promise or a frustration depending on your patience levels.

Mine, for the record, remain intact.

Because when Widow's Bay is operating at full capacity — misty, funny, genuinely unsettling, and timed to perfection — it is exactly the kind of television that makes you resent having other things to do with your evening.

The curse of Widow's Bay, it turns out, is that you cannot leave.

Which, for the tourism initiative, is actually quite promising.
