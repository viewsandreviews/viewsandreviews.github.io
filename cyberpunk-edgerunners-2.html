Anime Review

Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2

Night City Was Always the Point
Studio Trigger
Year 2026
Episodes 10

Studio Trigger had set the bar.
Then they handed themselves
the pole.

There are second seasons, and then there are second seasons. The kind that arrive dragging a shadow so long it blocks out most of the light before a single frame has played. Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2 is that second kind — a follow-up to one of the most emotionally devastating anime releases in recent memory, a show that turned a video game tie-in into a genuine gut punch and left an entire corner of the internet inconsolable for weeks. Studio Trigger had set the bar. Then they handed themselves the pole.

The biggest weakness of this second season stems from its title. Specifically its last part.

Coming in second — especially after the gold medal performance of Season 1 — gave little to no chance for this competently written follow-up to survive the comparison. Because this isn't really a sequel in the traditional sense. It's not a tragic hero's journey continued, but intersected tales from Night City — separate lives that share a city, a sky, and a doomed trajectory. Cyberpunk: Night City Stories doesn't have the same ring, granted. But it would have bought the season the goodwill it deserved and never received.

✦

That framing matters, because the season is built differently from the ground up. Where Season 1 followed David's singular, inexorable fall, Edgerunners 2 spreads its attention across an ensemble of characters who each arrive at Night City's table with their own stack of chips and their own way of losing them. Roman is a young cinephile recording the city's unfiltered stories from the ground level — an outsider's eye in a place that chews up observers. Talia, raised in the corporate machine, throws herself into Maelstrom with the particular ferocity of someone rejecting everything they were handed. D is a nomadic netrunner hunting down the killer of his clan, dragging the weight of the dead through neon-lit corridors. And then there's Weak Kingsley — a veteran edgerunner living in the shadow of his former glory, which in Night City means living in a shadow that only grows.

It's a structure that takes longer to earn your investment, but when it does, it pays out. These lives begin at a distance from each other and gradually pull into orbit, converging with real craft toward a finale that draws a stream of tears almost as strong as Season 1's.

Almost. You'll note we said almost.

✦
✦

The uneven character development makes sure of that. King gets the short end of the stick in a way that's difficult to ignore. For a character with that much latent weight — the veteran who outlived his own relevance, the man who knows exactly how this city disposes of people like him — he deserves more than the margins. He occupies space without quite occupying the narrative, and in a season built around the convergence of fates, a character who never fully lands his half of the equation leaves the whole thing slightly lopsided.

The others fare better. Talia's arc from corporate product to Maelstrom true believer carries a specific kind of tragedy — not the tragedy of falling, but of choosing the fall and meaning it. Roman, meanwhile, provides the season's conscience: someone recording Night City's brutality rather than participating in it, which turns out to be its own form of complicity. Their stories, and the way they eventually intersect with D's hunt for revenge, form the season's real spine. It doesn't announce itself loudly. But when it clicks, it genuinely clicks.

✦

What keeps pulling you through regardless is Night City itself — and what makes the season's weaker stretches so revealing. When the city recedes into the background and we're left alone with the characters in quieter, more insular moments, the story starts to feel curiously empty. It's a reminder that Night City has always been the central character. For however questionably logical this dystopian hellscape is, it's the neon aesthetics of a fever dream that keep dragging us back. The people, except for David — the right one — are mostly a bonus.

The show understands this in its DNA, which is why it never insults you with false hope. No happy endings in Night City. One oligarchy falls, another rises. The world grinds on indifferently, and the season's best moments lean into that — the feeling that your characters are insects under glass, vivid and briefly luminous, running out of time in a city that will not notice when they're gone.

✦

Less honest is what the finale does with that darkness. The closing act leans too hard into the emo teenager energy that sits at CyberPunk lore's worst tendencies, mistaking brooding for depth in a way that slightly undercuts what the buildup earned. There are moments that should land as tragedy and instead land as theatre — gestures toward feeling rather than the thing itself.

And the increased budget, for all its promise, still hasn't fixed the animation problem. There are more still shots than a Stephen Hawking documentary. For a series built on kinetic excess — on the visual language of bodies pushed past their limits, chrome and blood and speed — it remains a baffling choice. A show about people who modify themselves into weapons of motion shouldn't spend this much time standing still.

Edgerunners 2 is competent, at times genuinely moving television. It just had the misfortune of sharing a name with something great.
