Film Review

Sound of Freedom

A Toy-Gunned Call for Arms
Director Alejandro Gómez Monteverde
Year 2023
Starring Jim Caviezel, Mira Sorvino, Bill Camp

A story that needed to be told.
A story that needed to be heard.
A story that has all and nothing to do with this film.

2014 was the year. I was navigating, as per usual, through the webpages of a once inspirational website — today a dumpster for articles not even worthy of Buzzfeed publication. That's when I stumbled upon a short, self-written biography. A diary of sorts, in which the confessions were not of oneself, but of the sins of the world.

It was a young lady's confession — not much older than my university-goer self. But while I struggled with the struggles of every engineering student, she had a deeper, personal struggle to share: the struggles of a survivor.

Lydia, as I will call her, grew up in that infamous, flag-waving, meth-infested underbelly of the USA. Her parents figured out that there was one service that paid more than giving blowjobs in exchange for meth coupons. A service that involved their then-infant Lydia. These services didn't stop post-infancy. All of Lydia's pre-adult life was marked by all kinds of exploits, which she was able to describe with the minimum amount of graphic detail but with utmost sincerity and appeal to humanity. Lydia's story did have a happy ending. But the suffering described in that short, amateur article has echoed in my memories ever since, mixed with feelings of sympathy and anger.

It was a story that needed to be told — one that needed to be heard.

✦

A story that has all and nothing to do with Sound of Freedom. Because all is what needs to be told about both stories. And nothing is what Alejandro Gómez Monteverde was able to accomplish — in the cinematic sense. The young director's call for arms missed a few too many targets to stand out from a sea of peanut-budgeted action movies. His Sound of Freedom navigates between B-level crime thriller clichés at its best and Dhar Mann-styled appeals to virtue signalling at its worst.

But lack of sincerity, or altruism, does not seem to be at fault here — or so one hopes. What may be a genuine attempt to enlighten the masses becomes a less entertaining Taken, and protrudes itself no further than God's Not Dead quality of preaching.

✦

The acting performances are as much of a culprit as the unfleshed script itself. The actors share the dung-trophy of fault with a screenplay of quality only comparable to 2000s pop-rock music videos. We're talking about Donkey Kong's levels of character development. And not any post-90s Kongs either.

Even Jim Caviezel's strong lead performance cannot survive this self-sabotage. His protagonist, special agent Tim Ballard, tries to represent the conflict between accepting the reality of human nature's evil persuasion and the duty to push back against these bottomless pits — only to end up a caricatured hero moved by anime-levels of power of vindictive love.

The last minutes, dedicated to tracing the unfolded story to its real events, provide the only sincere fright. It reminds us that the tragic story is as much of a reality as it is a sad execution.

✦

I realise the judgement I cast is severe and contemptuous. But before any feelings of pity to praise surface, I remind myself of the story of Lydia. One that deserves justice, both on and off-screen. One that I hope one day will be shown with the eyes of a survivor, and the determination of a fighter.
