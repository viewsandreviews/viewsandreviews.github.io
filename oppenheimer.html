Film Review

Oppenheimer

Or How I Learned to Start Worrying and Hate the Bomb
Director Christopher Nolan
Year 2023
Starring Cillian Murphy, Robert Downey Jr., Emily Blunt

The Rhyno of Dyno.
The Clifford of the C4.
The Shroom of the Boom.

If everything could be achieved, who has the authority to create and who to destroy? If everything is possible, how could there ever be a right or wrong?

The 1% of floss-surviving germs are believed to have founded QAnon.

These are but samples of inner, echoing voices, as Christopher Nolan's latest Odyssey metastasizes in our minds. Internalized narrations that are hopefully spontaneous, as that is the hope of art. But art can rarely be achieved without an imprint of pride, and pride never lacks in a Nolan film.

✦

More of an epic, as this Odyssey named after its own Enea brings us back to the (sometimes) black-and-white past of Oppenheimer. An elegantly aged Cillian Murphy takes the lead, after making a career of show-stealing secondary roles. Murphy makes a statement, with a stoic performance that is daring in a time of assured excess.

A Cato stuck in the limbo of scientific morality, whose will is moved by ethnic and political identity. His story is both new and old, as resolution by violence is not a new chapter in history, but harnessing the power of the atom is. A power that goes beyond human imagination, or human responsibility. A power greater than any creator, and Murphy perfectly channels the oxymoron of the foolish genius.

✦

Murphy's performance is even more impressive when scrolling through the quasi-infinite list of A-listers forming this impressive cast. His genius, playboy, philanthropist ires the envy of a post-Tony Stark Robert Downey Jr. Definitely not the most accurate description of our protagonist, but an unexpectedly realistic characterization.

Downey Jr. is at his first "villainous" role, although no one can be a villain against the father of the Bomb. No villains and no victors in this tragic recalling of recent pasts. A reflection that had been conserved in the annals of history, only to be forgotten by modern greed.

✦

Nolan had already given us a glimpse of his unusually more somber approach to history, with both tone and quality matching those of Dunkirk. What's new in this latest attempt is the lack of his contradistinguished Fi in Sci-fi. Instead, what we are delivered is sometimes a procedural drama, sometimes a timepiece, while always remaining a biopic about our own god of death.

Die-hard Nolanites won't struggle to notice the other common watermarks of the director. First and foremost, the segmentary timeline, drunk of flashbacks and flashforwards. One of his favored expedients, although never matched in quality from his first attempt in Memento. It works more, instead, as an expedient to flock us back to the cinemas, as the flashed memories of past and present are as engaging as they are convoluted.

Streams of colors and beams of lights pulse together, scattered through the film; alternated to a melancholic look at how the past of brilliant minds is infested with problems all too common to be alienating. Unlike theoretical physics, the themes of ethics, as well as its burdens, remain the same. Right and wrong, life and death.

✦

Oppenheimer may very well be Nolan's biggest technical achievement, a Herculean feat for the ever-daring director. That said, it is not his most impressive feat. This I say with the risk of hurting the prideful Nolan, who undoubtedly demonstrates an improved mastery of his new elements. His bombastic sound is finally matched to the fiery shows put on screen. The constant diatribes between the characters are his most mature yet.

In all honesty, it is his most perfect achievement. But as nature has shown, it is often those little imperfections, those exaggerated impressions and squiggly lines that linger in our palates. Those that keep us talking. But after 3+ hours of movie, talking is out of the question for my tired body.

And while I take a nap, remember to not destroy the world. Or you may regret it. Maybe.
