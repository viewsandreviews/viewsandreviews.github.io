Manga Review

Chainsaw Man

A Freudian Shōnen with a (Demonic) Heart
Author Tatsuki Fujimoto
Published 2018–2020 (Part 1)
Genre Shōnen, Action, Dark Fantasy

Chainsaw man's answer always remains simple, yet never banal.
Sometimes all we wish for are simple solutions to convoluted problems.

Have you ever fantasized about slashing all of your most loathed foes at the thundering roar of a blazing chainsaw coming out of your head? You haven't, you say? What is wrong with me, you say? While I continue searching for an answer to the last question, let me make a case for why you should give Chainsaw Man a try.

Chainsaw Man is not a movie, but a manga instead; the first of many mangas I will be reviewing in this mostly neglected blog of mine. And yes, I am indeed a weeb, and now that the dark ages of oppression against my kin have finally ceased, I'm here to share what I love about the outrageously moronic stories narrated in these Japanese comics.

✦

Chainsaw Man is a shōnen manga. What is a shōnen, you ask, implicitly flaunting your fulfilling social life? A shōnen is either the highest form of entertainment a 13-year-old may feel like coming across, or the form of entertainment the same 13-year-old would disdain most after reaching his elitist tween years.

Much of the pleasure I still find in reading the occasionally original pages of shōnen stems from the same love I first developed watching the golden age of Japanese cinema. The over-dramatization, over-acting and comical straightforwardness in theme narration and exposition were all blissfully enchanting attributes that made me fall for Nipponic films. And luckily, all of these secret ingredients have transpired into the hand-drawn pages of Japanese comics.

Chainsaw Man beautifully mixes all of those tasty ingredients while adding a bit more, while still ending up being a bit less.

✦

Chainsaw Man tells the tale of itsy-bitsy Denji, poorest of poor, most suffering of emos. Carrying over the debt left on him by his good-for-nothing deceased father, little Denji still cheers on through his daily survival struggles accompanied by his little demonic, chainsaw-headed puppy, Pochita.

This world Denji is trying to survive in is inhabited by devils, all stemming from mankind's greatest fears, and Denji, like many others, makes a living hunting these demonic creatures. For however well-wished and capable he may be, poor Denji ends up always left with unflavored crumbs of cash, while kept being exploited by his yakuza loan sharks.

Right when all of his plain dreams seemed to be vanishing, after the yakuza organizes a ploy to get rid of him, Pochita decides to offer him a deal. Little doggo offers to become Denji's new heart, and in exchange all he asks for is that Denji will show him the pleasures of living the most regular of lives. Denji soon realizes that this new heart of his came with a surprise Black Friday deal package, which included a sweet set of meat-grinding machinery. And Chainsaw Denji does not hesitate one second to try them out on his foes.

✦

Much of the manga follows his MacGyver-ish exploits on how to creatively use his newly found powers. If you started worrying the story may fall into the pit of gratuitous slash-and-dash entertainment, do not fret. Denji has another newly found life-or-death mission: grabbing a handful of Tig Ol' Bitties. I mean, we're talking about a 14-year-old with an apparatus that would make John Holmes blush. Give the kid a break.

Poor, goofily dumb, horny and cheerful protagonist; cute little four-legged sidekick; sudden supernatural powers. All of this, as the nerdier of you may have already noticed, does not deviate from the habitual clichés of shōnen. Even its most contradistinguishing feature — the chainsaw installed in the titular character's head — is nothing more than a bloodier twist from previous mangas such as No Guns' Life; which themselves are nothing but a twist from human-beast hybrid mythos.

✦

Fortunately, the guts-filled action of Chainsaw Man is not its only redeeming quality. Chainsaw Man's world is undoubtedly more complex than its protagonist's simple desires, which is immediately noted after being introduced to the other main characters.

While no longer exploited by the yakuza, Denji is now a pet/servant to Makima, head of the Hunter division in charge of defeating the devils in Japan. Makima's schemes are labyrinthically complex enough to leave both other characters and viewers anxious and concerned, with the exclusion of Minotaur Denji. All he wants is to be loved by Makima, and possibly get a taste of her not-so Tig Ol' Bitties.

Makima's schemes are not the only engaging intricacies explored in the story; all of the main supporting characters are well-developed and examined to further extend the web of thrills spun by author Tatsuki Fujimoto. It's almost as if this shōnen protagonist was catapulted into the stories of other, more mature and intricate manga protagonists.

✦

Still, Denji manages to remain both at the center of the story and of our attention during the whole span of the show. Denji's goals remain mostly unchanged throughout the various arcs of plotting, espionage and back-stabbing. Be it his immutable, ironclad will or his flat-earth levels of ignorance, Denji almost always and unavoidably falls into the trap of all of his predators. And he always makes it out alive through some bizarre albeit charming measure, thanks to his chainsaw-armed shōnen plot armor and simplistic moral convictions.

Fujimoto cleverly avoids making the mistake of giving clear answers to the bigger questions the other main characters ask to Denji and to themselves. Often, those deterministic ultimatums given by authors end up taking away so much of our freedom as readers to enjoy these silly stories, and to freely add any level of intellectual intricacy as we may please.

Chainsaw Man's answer always remains simple, yet never banal. Sometimes all we wish for are simple solutions to convoluted problems. And Chainsaw Man is a kind reminder that, sometimes, the best solution to all the overly complex challenges we may be going through is to simply grab a chainsaw and go full Patrick Bateman.
