Film Review

Sinners

The Blackest Collab of Them All, and Coogler's Best Work Yet
Director Ryan Coogler
Year 2025
Starring Michael B. Jordan, Hailee Steinfeld, Miles Caton

The '80s were signed by the mafioso tales of Scorsese and De Niro.
The '90s: Burton and Depp revitalizing the femboy aesthetics.
The 2000s got us (milk)shaken by PTA and Daniel Day-Lewis.
And in the present, we finally add color to the screen
with the blackest collab of them all —
Ryan Coogler and Michael B. Jordan.

Sinners — the ultimate Blaxploitation blockbuster, first of its kind, son of many. The latest Coogler–Jordan collaboration, on a mission to make black the new… black.

The film opens on a train car clanking through the Jim Crow South. Twin protagonists captained by Jordan — fever dream of 95% of female and 65% of male audiences — Elijah "Smoke" and Elias "Stack" Moore are fleeing the rot of Chicago's criminal underworld, only to land in the slow-burning decay of their Mississippi hometown, where ghosts are real, and not all of them wear sheets.

✦

Smoke and Stack didn't come back just to haunt their childhood homes — they came with a dream. A juke joint. Not just any dive bar, but a revival: Black-owned, Black-celebrating, and soul-soundtracked by the ghosts of the Delta blues themselves. They want to build something defiant, something alive — a place where the community can breathe without watching their back or softening their voice. Something sacred, in a land that's tried to scrub out the sacred for generations.

Coogler takes a page from Jordan Peele, and a whole screenplay from Rodriguez and Tarantino, to bring out his best work yet. A work founded in themes, and contoured by enough action tropes to keep the masses aboard.

✦

Let's start with the most on the nose: segregation.

The film makes segregation tangible. Lines aren't just drawn on signs — they're etched in where you can scream, dance, or even exist. A Black juke joint in a white county is a miracle and a provocation. The vampires don't just come at night. They come when Black joy dares to be visible.

Led by the tap-dancing Irishman Remmick, they come not just to take part — they come to take over. The juke joint they've built becomes more than a venue. It's a sanctuary: part speakeasy, part church, part vampire trap.

Here, blood isn't just blood. It's heritage, ancestry, stolen history — weaponized. The vampires in Sinners aren't outsiders. They're insiders who blend, mimic, and consume. They talk gospel, but they feast on culture. They wear the music, the slang, the sorrow — and drain it dry.

✦

Next: religion.

Religion in Sinners is both refuge and restraint. Sammie, the preacher's son and a walking contradiction with a voice like holy whiskey, straddles that line. His father prays with a Bible in one hand and fear in the other, preaching salvation while the world outside refuses to save them. The church becomes a pressure cooker: a place to cry, confess, and conform. But also a place to hide — from sin, from truth, and from the reality that the holy isn't always just.

That's where Coogler's vampires break the stereotypical Christian fear of God. In a speech that arrives with terrible timing, Remmick casts his anger toward the divine realm — but only the one found on earth, which constrained him as it constrained so many, subjugating masses with delusions of righteousness.

✦

Finally: assimilation.

Assimilation in Sinners isn't a slow drift. It's a hunger. The push to become respectable, clean, God-fearing — and ultimately palatable — becomes a death sentence. The ones who survive are those who remember: you can't sanitize struggle. You either own your roots, or watch them be used against you.

✦

While the thematic drives remain strong throughout, the film isn't without its laughter and excitement. Coogler knows that even in hell, there's room for jokes. The script dances like the music it celebrates — bending genre, skipping beats, but always keeping rhythm.

Visually, it's a Southern Gothic mixtape: molasses moonlight, sweat-stained linen, shotgun churches, and jukeboxes that sing like they've seen too much. But the true excitement comes from the sound. Ludwig Göransson — a new John Williams at this point — blends Delta blues, trap, and Gregorian chants like he's summoning something. And he is.

The guitar Sammie plays isn't just an instrument — it's a relic of resistance, an heirloom of pain and pride, strummed in defiance of silence and assimilation. The glue that brings the living and the dead together is also what makes Sinners as great as it is.

✦

If there's a minor bruise on Sinners' otherwise rich, bruised apple of a narrative, it's that its last act occasionally slips into autopilot — where explosions speak louder than ghosts, and fists fly a bit too fast for feelings to keep up. The bullet ballet is slick, sure, but we've seen some of these dance moves before, in a dozen lesser movies with fewer things to say.

Still, Sinners earns its indulgence. Because when a film's soul is this well-tuned, a few familiar bangs don't drown out the gospel.
