Film Review

Materialists

A Spreadsheet for the Heart, Checked but Not Delivered
Director Celine Song
Year 2025
Starring Dakota Johnson, Pedro Pascal, Chris Evans

Love is transactional,
but we get to decide its currency.

Celine Song returns all too early from her Icarian highs of Past Lives, only to re-encapsulate her familiar vision of love into a heightened direct-to-streaming chick flick.

This time we move to present — a present far too acquainted — with Materialists (2025), an attempt to elevate the rom-com formula with Courier-levels of breakdown.

Past Lives' Greta Lee is now Lucy — played by Dakota Johnson — a high-end matchmaker in New York whose job is love brokerage. She seats herself at opulence, pairing clients with partners based on height, income, hobbies, and heritage, ensuring the Bumble-esque filters are checked and delivered.

✦

Lucy is the quintessential working lady of the 21st Century. Her worldview is clean and transactional: marriages with spreadsheets, love with ROI. Song interjects some of her own personality into the character, with an undeniable and shared Asian immigrant experience playing into Lucy's worldview.

Workaholic Lucy has only time for relationships that will bring her a marble-adorned, 50 by 50 meter, grave companion.

Enter Harry (Pedro Pascal), the mythical "unicorn" of private equity wealth, and John (Chris Evans), her broke ex-boyfriend, still chasing acting dreams in the city that eats ambition for breakfast. Lucy will have to juggle and ultimately choose between the two Chads, as it is always the case for run-of-the-mill Big Apple ladies.

✦

Call it a fantasy, call it a cliché — I'll call it lazy writing. The rom-com obsession with love dichotomy surrounds yet again the walls and screentime of an otherwise competent movie.

The film does indeed play like a rom-com, stripped of its fluff, layered with class anxiety, gendered expectations, and societal judgment. Flashbacks expose Lucy's past relationship choices fueled by necessity and self-worth crises. She left John because he was a poor bet, but now wealth can't guarantee happiness.

That said, the fluff characteristic of the genre offers a familiar padding that usually helps land the over-the-top premises. That is the very first faux-pas of Materialists — it tries to diverge from the genre without concern for its requirements of comedy and pacing.

✦

Cinematographer Shabier Kirchner frames NYC like a luxe labyrinth: filtered sunlight, chic apartments, minimalist costumes — all so polished they feel intentionally alienating. The soundtrack by Daniel Pemberton, plus tracks by Japanese Breakfast, adds a mournful beat to the love triangle's tension.

Johnson channels guarded vulnerability — her Lucy is impeccably styled, fiercely competent, yet emotionally bankrupt. Pascal is quiet luxury incarnate: a man who offers stability, but not explanation. Evans, meanwhile, becomes heartbreak in slow burn: earnest, gentle, painfully real — a foil to Lucy's self-modernization.

The trio, while competent, does not always have the perfect on-screen chemistry — their chemical reactions less emotional combustions and more parsimonious idles.

✦

Both plot points and subplots feel hasty — particularly the client storyline that ends with trauma served without nuance. Song's touch is there, but the love and intimacy that characterized Past Lives is faded into a sort-of generic mildbuster attempt.

Her qualities as a screenwriter remain remarkable, with dialogues often superior to the scenes and flows of rigid, under-fleshed-out stories. Her script interrogates how we commodify love while ironically longing for its messy authenticity. Lucy's cynicism is earned, but not armor. It's a deflection from being seen.

Several are the stare-downs and stand-offs — something Song seems to cherish on a visual level. But not all that works once, works twice, especially when forced into the middle of an underdeveloped spot.

✦

The film's emotional payoff wobbles — the logic of Lucy's choices doesn't always add up — but moments like a rooftop dance sequence or a candid reunion scene give Materialists its emotional heft. We're left with a love triangle that didn't resolve neatly, but left scars we can feel.

Materialists doesn't offer romance, exactly — it offers a series of transactions, with people instead of invoices. And in that balance of class and heartache, cynicism and surprise, it reminds us that love can't be reduced to value, even when we try to price it that way.

In a world selling love like real estate, Materialists asks: what if you'd rather fail than cash in?

So go on and give it an attempted viewing — worst-case scenario, you may just find your perfect DIY anniversary gift idea.
