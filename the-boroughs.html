TV Review

The Boroughs

Stranger Things for People Who Have Stopped Understanding the World
Network Netflix
Year 2026
Starring Alfred Molina, Geena Davis

There was a time when television believed children were the best protagonists for stories about wonder.
That time is gone.
In this vacuum, it is an unlucky group of retired Scooby-Doo cosplayers that is rounded up to defeat demogorgons.

A sleepy neighborhood. Strange occurrences. Eccentric residents uncovering mysteries lurking beneath ordinary life. The synth-heavy atmosphere, the suburban Americana dipped in supernatural unease, the "ordinary people stumble into extraordinary horror" structure — it's all here.

Where The Boroughs deviates from the Netflix epic it clearly draws inspiration from is in replacing childhood innocence with latehood regret. These characters aren't discovering the world; they're realizing they stopped understanding it decades ago.

And that's where the show finds its own identity.

✦

Shared with Stranger Things is also the casting director's talent for bringing underappreciated actors out of blockbuster retirement and giving them a chance at redemption.

Enter Alfred Molina, finally holding the power of boomer rage in the palm of his hands. His distraught Sam Cooper captains the gang of elderly mystery solvers, with the amount of rage, regret, and penance you'd expect from the generation that allowed for turtlenecks under blazers.

Even Geena Davis' alarmingly botoxed visage plays her role with more emotional versatility than the filler-filled S5 Ms. Bobby Brown.

The chemistry between the ensemble feels lived-in rather than manufactured — which is crucial when the show spends half its runtime watching elderly people argue about supernatural events with the same aggression reserved for neighbourhood parking disputes. Even when the dialogue occasionally stumbles into streaming-era exposition soup, the actors carry it with enough sincerity, and just enough self-awareness, to keep the emotional core intact.

Every conversation feels layered with decades of passive aggression, unresolved friendships, and medication side effects. They bicker not like television characters, but like people who have shared barbecue space for twenty years and secretly resent each other's gardening habits.

✦

There's also something unmistakably Up-coded buried underneath the mystery. The series understands aging in the same melancholic way Pixar did: as a slow accumulation of unrealized plans, routines, and quiet loneliness disguised as normalcy.

When the show leans into comedy, it does so from the point of view of those who have seen their grandparent stumble and yell at clouds — or of those finally ready to yell at those lizard weather-controlled nimbles themselves.

✦

Unfortunately, The Boroughs also suffers from the disease currently infecting nearly every streaming series: the obligation to become content instead of story.

The pacing drags in the middle stretch, where episodes begin circling mysteries instead of progressing them. Characters repeatedly have conversations that feel less like human interaction and more like writers desperately preserving material for future seasons.

You can practically hear the Netflix algorithm whispering from behind the camera: "Leave some unanswered questions. We may need Season 4."

The dialogue swings between genuinely sharp character moments and exposition that sounds like placeholder text accidentally left in the final draft. Entire subplots exist less because they enrich the narrative and more because serialization now demands perpetual narrative elasticity.

Nobody is allowed closure anymore. Closure doesn't test well with subscription retention.

✦

And yet, despite these frustrations, the show remains adequately compelling. Because beneath the bloated pacing and occasionally overextended mythology lies something emotionally sincere: a story about people confronting the terrifying realization that life doesn't become simpler with age — only more tiresome.

The Boroughs may not reach the highs of Stranger Things, nor the emotional devastation of Up, but it occupies an oddly charming middle ground between them.

A suburban supernatural drama where nostalgia meets mortality, wrapped in enough mystery to keep you watching even while complaining about it.

Which, coincidentally, is also how most people describe adulthood.
