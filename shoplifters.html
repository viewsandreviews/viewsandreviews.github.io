Film Review

Shoplifters

The Kind of Love That Doesn't Ask Permission
Director Hirokazu Kore-eda
Year 2018
Starring Lily Franky, Sakura Ando, Kirin Kiki

"Sometimes you choose family.
Sometimes you shoplift it."

The reality of Japan is one of traditions, culture, and rules. In the mean streets of uptown Tokyo, you will find an orderly line for a mostly empty karaoke bar, an excess of gloves for any service job, an extra bow for the smallest inconvenience.

In Japan, everything has its place — and if you don't, people politely pretend not to see you.

Kore-eda doesn't pretend. He zooms in.

✦

Here, in this quietly explosive film, the societal veil of politeness is ripped with the gentlest touch. Shoplifters is not just about theft. It's about survival. It's about the kind of love that doesn't ask permission. The kind of family you build from scraps.

On paper, the plot is deceptively clean: a family living in the margins of Tokyo takes in a neglected little girl. They steal food. They share baths. They're warm. Until they're not. Until the truth arrives — not in a twist, but like a slow leak in a fragile roof. You don't notice it until you're already wet.

What begins as a tale of a found family turns into a slow-burning tragedy where the definitions of legality and morality crack apart. What is family? What is a crime? When does an act of love become an act of harm?

✦

There's something about Shoplifters that cuts deeper when you've just walked through the efficiency and order of modern Japan. A country obsessed with perfection — emotional stoicism presented as strength, shame treated like currency.

And here we are, watching people who live outside of that order. People who aren't efficient. Who aren't neat. Who aren't ashamed, or maybe they are, but in a language that isn't always spoken aloud. The type of Japanese people you can only find in a shoe-sized izakaya on a weekday night, after the third shot of shōchū breaks their formal barriers. Until the morning after, at least.

✦

Shoplifters quietly exposes how the worth of a person in Japanese society — and many others — is tied to their function. Your job. Your role. Your output. If you fall outside of that? You become invisible. Disposable.

Each character carries a different scar from that invisibility: the grandmother living off a pension from a dead husband; the woman discarded from love; the man who can't hold a job; the boy who wonders if stealing is the only way to be seen. And yet, they build something meaningful. Something beautiful. They're not symbols. They're people — flawed, tender, real.

You start to see how sometimes dignity isn't given. Sometimes you have to steal it.

✦

What makes a family? Love? Survival? Shared secrets? A hiding place from the world?

What makes Shoplifters so masterful is that it asks these questions through the smallest gestures: a shared meal, a silent glance, a stolen shirt, a held hand. No grand speeches. No orchestral swells. Just the sound of a world that keeps spinning, even when it leaves its kindest people behind.

Kore-eda doesn't give us a resolution. He gives us a reflection — one you don't find in a mirror, but in the eyes of someone who sees you, even when society refuses to.
