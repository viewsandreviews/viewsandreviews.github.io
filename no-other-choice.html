Film Review

No Other Choice

Korean Cinema's Long, Proud Tradition of Turning Social Rot into Premium Entertainment
Director Park Chan-wook
Year 2025
Starring Lee Byung-hun, Son Ye-jin

There's something uniquely comforting about Korean cinema:
no matter how bad your life is, theirs is always worse.
No Other Choice continues that long, proud tradition —
a civic duty done with style, impeccable tailoring,
and at least one morally bankrupt authority figure per frame.

Park's blackly comic riff on the job market as crucible is a sufficiently funny, albeit sometimes logorrheic take on what happens when dignity gets outsourced.

Inspired by Donald E. Westlake's The Ax, the film works with a simply elaborate(d) premise: a veteran paper-industry expert, humiliated and unemployed, decides the quickest way to secure a position is to remove his competition by Wile E. Coyote-ing unfortunate beep-beeps.

When life throws you anvils, well then you're pretty fucked.

✦

The plot unfolds like a corporate horror-comedy pitched at a shareholders' meeting: Man-su (Lee Byung-hun) is laid off, humiliated in interviews, and slowly transmutes from mild-mannered professional into a man who sees colleagues as liabilities.

Lee Byung-hun carries this tonal tightrope like a man who can jest at Theranos shareholders' meetings. His Man-su is pathetic, sympathetic, infuriating, and oddly believable — the exact mixture you need when you're rooting for a protagonist who spends most of his screen time plotting quietly depraved strategies.

Son Ye-jin as his wife Mi-ri brings the resigned domesticity that makes his decisions feel less like psychopathy and more like a slow, social pressure-cooked collapse. That same thread is then carried over to the rest of the pitiful middle-class presented by Park.

✦

What Park does with more precision is building a Machiavellian reality like he never has before: interviews become interrogations; networking events are theater for predators; résumés read like hit lists. The film's tonal bravado — at once farcical and viscous — forces you to laugh and then immediately check your own LinkedIn.

Steady of hand, steady of eye. Frames so crisp you could subpoena them. The film's aesthetic is a chaos that can only be replicated by reality.

✦

But let's be blunt: the film's social critique occasionally smells like a very well-packaged sermon, coming from a pastor who's never had to iron his robe.

The satire of modern work life is potent, but also sometimes performative — Park holds up the horrors of unemployment as if pointing out the obvious to an audience who already nodded at the café. There are moments when the film's rage feels curated for maximum festival-appreciation: clever, caustic, and occasionally a touch derivative — like a tirade you'd find in a think-piece titled "How Capitalism Kills Your Soul (And How Film Will Help You Process It)."

In plain terms: the movie nails the problem but often flirts with saying it a little too loudly, as if to make sure we don't miss the point.

✦

Still, when the film lands, it does so with style. Park manages to be mordant and humane in the same breath — funny one moment, morally nauseating the next.

At its best, No Other Choice is a paradox: a savage satire that still makes room for empathy. Park refuses to merely condemn his protagonist; he understands the delicious, awful logic that pushes a quiet man toward monstrous acts. The film's moral dizziness and technical bravura keep you glued.

You'll leave equal parts entertained, unsettled, and suspicious of anyone who lists "team player" at the top of their résumé.

So yes: go see it — if only to be reminded that in 2025, the most terrifying thing isn't a robot replacing your job. It's a human who decides he wants to break free from the corpo-matrix, but has as only guidance Andy Lew Tate.
