Film Review

Sentimental Value

Puzzles You Never Solved Don't Disappear — They Reproduce
Director Joachim Trier
Year 2025
Starring Stellan Skarsgård, Renate Reinsve, Inga Ibsdotter Lilleaas

There are two types of writers.
The ones who write because they have something to say,
and the ones who write because they want to find what to say.
Joachim Trier is the third. The adventurer.

Where he adventures feels awfully familiar — both for long-distance fans, but especially for more proxy ones like yours truly. Those Oslo neighborhoods amp up the sentimental value that one may experience while talking about our closest neighbors. Family.

✦

Sentimental Value feels like a mid-career film in the most telling sense. Not a breakthrough, not a farewell, but a pause. A reflection. A filmmaker looking back at the world he's filmed before, after being exposed to a new one, only to realize the new isn't all that different from the old.

The story centers on Gustav — Stellan Skarsgård, once again looking like a man who has disappointed people professionally — a once-revered filmmaker whose greatest legacy might be emotional absenteeism. His daughters orbit him like estranged satellites: Nora (Renate Reinsve), an actress with just enough success to feel hollow, and Agnes (Inga Ibsdotter Lilleaas), the quieter sibling who carries family history the way some people carry glass — carefully, never opening the box.

Gustav tries to bond with his daughters through creation, hoping to resolve the same fractures that scarred him.

But puzzles you never solved don't disappear — they reproduce. They resurface as inheritance. As expectation. As silence passed down like heirlooms.

✦

The "sentimental value" of the title is not nostalgia — it's emotional debt.

The guise of art proposed by Gustav is parallel to artistic expression everywhere: often just a timid man's way of shouting what hurts, without raising his voice. Sometimes without words. Sometimes without even knowing what he's saying.

Sentimental Value understands that. The story is so emotionally loud that you barely notice the silence behind it — until Trier wants you to. And then it hits.

✦

Art becomes a proxy for confrontation. The belief that if we examine our wounds creatively, we won't have to touch them directly. This cross-pollination between art and intimacy is as old as art itself — and as safe as the oldest profession. You confront the uncomfortable without ever leaving the comfort zone.

And it is with a touch of irony that Trier confronts his most emotion-heavy load in the comfort of a place he knows best.

But it's in this comfort we often give our best — and that's exactly what Trier does, aided by his army of veterans and familiar collaborators.

One new addition that goes well noticed: Inga Lilleaas, with a performance that, though quieter, may leave the deepest mark. Timid and loud at once, even in stillness, she turns Agnes into both the overlooked half of shared trauma and a continuation of her father's.

✦

As the journey completes, Trier leaves us where he always does: wondering. Not about answers, but about the necessity of losing ourselves — and whether we should ever be afraid of doing so.

Spoilers: we should.

But being afraid has its own sentimental value.

Queue the credits.
