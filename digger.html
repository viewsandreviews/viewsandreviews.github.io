A $125 million magic trick about oil, ice and art — and the critics who hate being the volunteer pulled up on stage.
Film Review

Digger

Now You See Him
Director Alejandro G. Iñárritu
Year 2026
Runtime 129 min
Starring Tom Cruise, Riz Ahmed, John Goodman, Sandra Hüller

A magician never reveals his secrets.

What he may do is trick you into thinking he will, when in reality it's all part of the show.

Alejandro González Iñárritu has spent the better part of a year doing exactly that. Trailers that explained nothing. A Tom Cruise buried under so much latex that the man reportedly brought a stopwatch to the make-up chair to get it under an hour. Posters that look like a Rothko was asked to sell a shovel.

Then the film opened, nobody knew what they were watching, and the magician took a bow anyway.

✦

Digger Rockwell (Cruise) is an aging oil magnate with a one-eyed cat called Maggie, a half-sister played by Sandra Hüller, and a President of the United States (John Goodman) somewhere between his speed dial and his back pocket. Together they drill in Greenland, ignore every methane warning their head of environmental safety (Riz Ahmed) puts in front of them, and dislodge an ice shelf that then sails, with admirable Scandinavian punctuality, straight towards Norway.

As a resident of Oslo, I would like to thank the screenwriters for granting us three days' notice. It is more than Ruter has ever given me.

What follows is at times the unexpected sequel to Harold & Kumar, complete with a President so sedated after rectal surgery that his solution to a rogue glacier is to nuke China. Other times it is the love child of Yorgos Lanthimos and Wes Anderson: Lanthimos's cruelty, Anderson's colour-blocked symmetry, raised by a Hollywood studio that insisted it grow up to be absurdism for the masses.

Cruise, meanwhile, is doing something he hasn't dared since Magnolia (and Tropic Thunder): being repellent on purpose. No running. No hanging off aeroplanes. Just a man, a shovel, a Texan drawl and a ten-minute monologue before the European Parliament that went through some seventy drafts — and which ends not with the room tearing him apart, but with the room tearing itself apart. Which, as anyone who has ever watched the actual European Parliament can confirm, is the most realistic thing in the film.

✦

I will not tell you what happens around the time the first act should be ending. Because that itself is part of the magic. And magic is best enjoyed in a room full of darkness and black people. You know what I'm talking about.

Iñárritu shows his skills with the arrogance of someone who knows very well where he belongs; an arrogance which, unsurprisingly, irritates those who can only ever critique and never create.

I say this as someone who exclusively critiques. [I once tried to create a sourdough starter. It died in four days. I wrote a review of it. Two stars, generous.]

This is, after all, the man who made Birdman — a film in which a theatre critic promises to bury a play before she has even seen it. Twelve years later the real critics turned up, split almost perfectly down the middle, and the studio responded by cutting an ad of Cruise yelling "Fuck those people!" over their quotes. Mr. Iñárritu does not need you to like his film. He needs you to have been in the room.

✦

The message is indeed partisan — this will not be screened at the next OPEC Christmas party — although it does keep the door slightly open to allow the other side to sneak a glance. Digger's defence is never that he is innocent. It is that he only ever sold what the rest of us were queuing up to buy.

And watching that from a country whose (very comfortable) welfare state is upholstered in petroleum, the door stayed open just long enough for a draft to reach my own pension fund.

In times of riches, art is a villain to boo and a bow to applaud. In times of peril, it is grief and comfort. One we rehearse before we are forced to perform it live.

But what it is ultimately about is the role of art during times of riches and times of peril — and the uncomfortable suspicion that, in both, art is mostly a way for the audience to feel something on their way to the exit.

In times of riches, it is a luxury: a satire to laugh at, a monster to cheer the death of, a curtain call to clap for until your hands hurt. In times of peril, it becomes something closer to a coping mechanism — a place to put the anger, the guilt, the people we have lost. Digger knows it is both. It is never ashamed of being either. But in its very best moments it lets that shame show on Cruise's face.

Is it too long? Yes. Too pleased with itself? Frequently. Do some of the jokes land with the grace of an ice shelf hitting Bergen? Absolutely.

But the film ends with an audience putting on their coats. I won't tell you what else they put on.

A magician never reveals his secrets.

He just lets you find out, somewhere between your seat and the exit, that you were the volunteer all along.
