Film Review

Project Hail Mary

The Best Space-Comedy of Any Decade
Directors Phil Lord, Christopher Miller
Year 2026
Starring Ryan Gosling

There are two kinds of people who dream about space.
The first look up at the stars and wonder what's out there.
The second hope to one day look down at Earth
to escape everyone currently living on it.

Project Hail Mary understands both impulses perfectly.

Directed by Phil Lord and Christopher Miller, with Drew Goddard adapting Andy Weir's aggressively dad-core novel, Project Hail Mary somehow achieves the impossible: it turns astrophysics into crowd-pleasing entertainment without making you feel like homework is due afterward.

✦

At its center is Ryland Grace, played by Ryan Gosling with the exact level of exhausted charisma modern cinema desperately survives on. Gosling has now perfected a very specific archetype: the man who looks permanently confused that he ended up in the middle of (his)tory.

Whether he's saving jazz, Barbie Land, or the literal human species, he approaches each catastrophe with the same energy of a substitute teacher unexpectedly handed military authority.

✦

The premise carries shades of Interstellar — Earth is dying, science must save us, humanity gathers under one last desperate project. But where Nolan reached for cosmic transcendence, Project Hail Mary does something far stranger: it makes space feel mundane while making human connection feel miraculous.

And that's the film's real trick.

The stars, the equations, the looming extinction event — all of it becomes background noise to the bizarre emotional core of the movie: companionship. The film introduces its communicative alien race with the same awkward warmth of someone trying to make friends at university orientation. What could have been pure spectacle instead becomes something deeply funny, oddly tender, and occasionally moving in a way that sneaks up on you.

✦

Goddard's screenplay thrives in this balance. Excessively overqualified, he understands outer worlds, but more importantly, he understands that audiences occasionally enjoy having fun. A radical concept in modern blockbuster filmmaking.

The humor lands constantly — not in the Marvel sense of interrupting emotion with irony, but in the older, better sense: comedy as survival mechanism. Characters joke because the alternative is screaming into the vacuum.

And yes, somehow, this may genuinely be one of the funniest big-budget films in years. Not because it's trying desperately to be memeable, but because absurdity naturally emerges when you place a socially awkward scientist in a tin can millions of miles from Earth and ask him to negotiate interspecies diplomacy with the Rock's premature expulsion.

✦

Visually, the film is just as gorgeous as the latest space dramas, without becoming sterile. Space isn't romanticized here — it's procedural. Lonely. Occasionally irritating. The true wonder lies elsewhere: in curiosity itself. In problem-solving. In the terrifying realization that intelligence and empathy might actually matter more than heroism.

And Gosling carries all of it beautifully. Botoxed into immortality, perhaps, but still unmistakably human underneath. He sells every beat: panic, loneliness, joy, terror, and the specific exhaustion of a man realizing he has become responsible for literally everyone.

✦

If the film stumbles, it's near the end. Grace's character arc begins to feel slightly overextended, as though the screenplay suddenly panics that audiences might leave without fully understanding him emotionally. The conclusion stretches itself one revelation too far, prolonging what was otherwise an almost perfectly calibrated finale.

Still, these are minor gravitational disturbances in what is otherwise the best space-comedy, film of the decade — arguably of any decade.

Humanity, appropriately enough, calls its final desperate attempt at survival a Hail Mary.

Cinema, this year, might have done the same.

Roll the curtains.
