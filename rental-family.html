Film Review

Rental Family

When You Perform Sincerity Long Enough, It Starts Leaving Residue
Director Hikari
Year 2023
Starring Brendan Fraser

Do you know the difference between a regular person and an actor?
Only an actor gets to kiss Angelina Jolie.
That's because Angelina Jolie only exists in movies.

In the real round world, her people call her Angie. Closer friends know her by other names still. But THE Angelina Jolie — the collective vision, the ever voluptuous Lara Croft — only exists in movies.

Or does she?

Let me introduce you to the newly voluptuous Brendan Fraser and his character in Rental Family.

✦

Philip — as he is sometimes called — is a self-described white fat man in Japan. One of many, dare I say, as my latest visit to the land of the rising perverted expats has confirmed.

The Japan he lives in is one of near-present: one where loneliness has achieved infrastructural status, where families are no longer built — they're subcontracted. Birthdays, weddings, parental pride, even mild disappointment: all available at competitive rates.

Philip operates as both employee and emotional hazard. He's a man whose job is to be convincing enough to pass as a father, son, or husband, while remaining hollow enough not to feel it afterward. The performance is key: play warmth like a learned language, fluent but uninhabited.

Spoilers: Philip is not very good at his job.

That is because Brendan Fraser cannot solely pretend to care.

✦

Hikari — also known as the Seal of directors — confirms not only her innate ability at picking cool stage names, but at picking her cast: often real-life extensions of the characters they are supposed to play.

Brendan Fraser, the actor, has much of Philip, the character. As he also had much of Charlie, in The Whale.

Because what can be more authentic than acting out one's life?

✦

If all this sounds like a solid recipe for a depressing tale of human identity, do not worry. Hikari brings the human in their whole(some)ness, humor included.

A humor that seeps in quietly, like mold. A child thanking a stranger for a memory he'll treasure forever. A man being paid to be disappointed in someone else's life choices. The film treats these moments with straight-faced seriousness, which somehow makes them funnier and crueler. You laugh, then immediately wonder what exactly you're laughing at, and whether it says something about you.

It does.

✦

What Rental Family nails is the transactional nature of modern intimacy. There is a profound bleakness in watching affection be timed, rated, and reviewed. And yet, the film never collapses into satire for satire's sake. It recognizes that these services exist not because people are shallow, but because they're desperate.

Capitalism didn't kill the family — it just figured out how to monetize its absence.

✦

As the story progresses, the lines begin to blur — because of course they do. When you perform sincerity long enough, it starts leaving residue. The protagonist begins to hesitate before clocking out. Not because he believes the illusion, but because he's forgotten what the alternative feels like.

The horror here isn't that fake families replace real ones. It's that real emotions adapt to the fake framework without complaint.

By the end, Rental Family leaves you with a deeply uncomfortable realization: authenticity might be overrated, but consistency isn't. And if someone shows up, remembers your name, and pretends to care on schedule, maybe that's not nothing.

Maybe that's just enough.
