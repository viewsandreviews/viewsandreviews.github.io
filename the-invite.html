Film Review

The Invite

One Bottle Becomes Three. Three Becomes a Reckoning.
Director Olivia Wilde
Year 2024
Starring Seth Rogen, Olivia Wilde, Edward Norton, Penélope Cruz

Two couples. One apartment.
Neighbours who are, by all available evidence,
having considerably more fun than you are.
The premise glitters. It winks at you.

It suggests an evening of delicious, consequence-free chaos. And then, quietly, it does something considerably more interesting than deliver on that promise.

But we'll get there.

✦

Joe and Angela (Seth Rogen and Olivia Wilde) are a married couple whose relationship has reached that far too familiar plateau where they are no longer fighting so much as conducting a permanent low-level archaeological dig through each other's grievances.

Their upstairs neighbours, the enigmatic and aggressively hip Marcos and Sofia, come down for dinner. One bottle becomes three. Three becomes a philosophical reckoning. The reckoning becomes something that none of the four people in the room were equipped to handle when the evening began.

The first half of The Invite is, not to put too fine a point on it, an absolute riot.

✦

Wilde weaponises the claustrophobia of the single location with the neuroticism of someone who has spent considerable time thinking about how to get rid of their partner, in the most silently painful manner. Norton plays a man so certain of his own enlightenment that the certainty has become its own form of insufferability. Cruz's Sofia operates on a different frequency entirely, all warmth and provocation and the unsettling sense that she may be way emotionally smarter than everyone in the room, while breaking all norms of civil society.

And Rogen plays Joe's particular brand of male bewilderment — a man being slowly outpaced by a version of adulthood he never quite agreed to — with a groundedness that is typical of his recent work, but also the one thing the film absolutely needs.

Without it the comedy tips into farce. With it, the farce tips into something that actually hurts a little. Which is, it turns out, the whole point.

✦

Because what The Invite is actually doing — underneath the extravagant promises of its first act, beneath the chaos and the laughter and the fourth bottle of wine — is the same thing it is asking Joe and Angela to do.

It lures you in with one film and then, when you are comfortable and laughing and have fully committed to the evening, it shows you another.

The neighbours are not a fantasy. They are a mirror. And what Joe and Angela see reflected in Marcos and Sofia is not the life they could be living, but the distance that has quietly opened up in the one they already have.

The film makes you reckon with that distance in the same moment it makes them reckon with it. Which is, when you think about it, a genuinely elegant trick.

✦

The gear change itself, however, is not always elegant. When the comedy recedes and the drama takes over, it does so with the subtlety of your drunken colleague suggesting tequila shots at a work after-hours — no crossfade, no warning, no transitional courtesy. The film keeps the comedy and the dread far too separate, so when the walls are knocked down, the whiplash can feel like a wet mess.

But it is not too long before we are dry.

And what stays with you after the credits roll and you have gathered your coat and your thoughts and whatever is left of your emotional composure is the realization that you came in expecting to watch two other people's marriage catch fire.

You leave checking on your own.
