Film Review

The Odyssey

Fortune Rewards the Strong. Nolan Rewards the Patient.
Director Christopher Nolan
Year 2026
Starring Matt Damon, Anne Hathaway, Tom Holland, Robert Pattinson

Fortune rewards the strong.
Nolan rewards the patient.

The Odyssey, in its almost three thousand years of living, has been many things. To some, a poem; to others, a proto-religious text. To many, a story. To even more, the story of all stories.

Translated, staged, adapted, and referenced so many times across so many centuries that you genuinely have to wonder what any filmmaker could possibly bring to it that Homer hadn't already covered.

Christopher Nolan's answer, characteristically, is: IMAX.

If you answered guns — well, too bad. Simpsons did it first.

✦

Two million feet of it, to be precise. But here's what's interesting about what Nolan actually does with all that canvas: he forgoes his signature foreshadowing. He abandons all but the most fundamental symbolism.

Because unlike Nolan's previous protagonists, Odysseus doesn't need a clever structure to be mythic. His story has already outlasted every structural innovation cinema has ever produced. All Nolan has to do is make you feel it.

And he does.

✦

Abundantly clear that Nolan cannot and will not be restrained by the handcuffs of mortal practical effects, he follows Odysseus in a journey to reach the top of the Olympus of blockbuster, scheming against the Spielbergs and Villeneuves. The original themes become the foundation: homecoming, hospitality, loyalty, divine justice — all showcased with effects as impractical for the stunt crew as they are feasts for the viewer's eyes.

But 2026 is the age, and a military-able population is dry. Odysseus cannot be the same call to arms, nor a symbol of ballistic perseverance that it once was. Thus motives have to be adjusted, but not renewed. And with a very expected twist of narrative, story and history become protagonists themselves.

✦

Matt Damon's Odysseus is not the Odysseus of Hollywood sandal-epics. He is tired. He is strategic. He is a sage whose intellect comes not from divine blessing but from the experience of a thousand adventures, wrapped up in a chronologically jagged two hours.

Anne Hathaway's Penelope — waiting at home while suitors circle with the patience of a family of Indians at an airport line — is quietly the film's moral spine. And Tom Holland's Telemachus, a boy who never really knew his father, searching for a man who may not resemble the legend he was raised on, gives the film an added resonant emotional thread.

All of them serve the story as if it were their master; never trying to surpass it. Always bowing to its majesty.

All but our favourite Gen Zer Robert Pattinson, whose Antinous — chief among Penelope's suitors — is further proof of his commitment to never playing a well-adjusted person ever again in his life, nor letting any of his peers out-act him in any shared scene.

Also, Travis Scott is in this film as a bard. The Odyssey was historically sung-and-rapped through. I will simply report this fact and allow you to form your own feelings about it.

✦

If the film has a legitimate complaint to answer, it arrives, surprisingly, in the first half. Nolan, faithful to the episodic structure of Homer's poem, moves through Odysseus's trials with the momentum of someone who has complete confidence in where he's going, and wants to go there as fast as possible — which is admirable, but occasionally punishing for an audience that hasn't been told the destination yet.

Without the connective tissue of what it all means, the early sequences land as spectacle before they land as story. Technically breathtaking. Emotionally deferred.

It is, not unlike the journey itself, a film that requires you to trust the direction before you can see the shore. But if there is anything Nolan has taught us over the years, it is that in him we can trust.

Those who do will find that the shore, when it arrives, is entirely worth the crossing. For it is a shore that feels like home after a long journey, although an uncomfortable feeling of unfamiliarity lingers.

Home is not the same, for we are not the same.

✦

Where The Odyssey will be seated in the Pantheon of Nolan classics is something only time will be able to tell. And even with Nolan not trying to claim the greatest legend as something of his own, he will be remembered as one of its great bards — with his magical device known as IMAX 70mm film.
