Film Review

Left-Handed Girl

No Villains Here. Just People Doing What They Were Taught.
Director Shih-Ching Tsou
Year 2025
Starring Janet Tsai

After many years working in the ins-and-outs of little Hollywood,
Shih-Ching Tsou makes her debut as prima donna of a feature-length film.
And if she's the prima, the D'Artagnan,
she finds her three musketeers — and directs them
in the way only she and long-time partner Sean Baker can.

Left-Handed Girl unfolds in the cracks of everyday life in Taipei — night markets, cramped apartments, half-spoken rules passed down like bad habits.

It's a film about a struggling mother and her two daughters, but really it's about inheritance: not money, not land, but expectations. The kind reserved for the non-protruding genitals' kind. The kind you don't agree to, yet somehow still obey.

✦

The titular "left-handedness" is less a trait than a sin. An inconvenience. A superstition dressed up as tradition.

The youngest daughter is corrected, redirected, gently punished — not out of cruelty, but out of love filtered through fear.

And that's where the film cuts deepest. No villains here. Just people doing what they were taught, hoping it will hurt less this time.

✦

The mother, played with raw exhaustion and quiet defiance by Janet Tsai, is the film's emotional axis. She is practical, loving, overwhelmed, and constantly negotiating between survival and tenderness.

But what anchors Left-Handed Girl emotionally are the performances of her two daughters, who operate on different emotional frequencies yet feel inseparable.

The youngest carries the film's most overt tension — her body still out of sync with the rules imposed on it. Every correction lands visibly.

The older sister, by contrast, performs adaptation. She's already learned when to bend, when to perform normalcy, when to disappear into routine. She has also decided that none of it makes sense — in a rebellious way that transcends teenagers around the world. Rebelling against her world, yet still submitting to the most powerful ruler: love.

Together, the trio embodies three stages of the same negotiation. Discovery. Wrath. Acceptance.

✦

Tsou, like Sean Baker before her, gravitates toward lives lived just outside the frame of the cinema of spectacles.

These aren't extraordinary struggles. Nor are they ordinary ones. They represent the margins where the left-out hang, in a canvas that had been made too slippery to them from the very start.

Economic precarity, cultural superstition, gendered expectations — none are dramatized as crises. They simply exist, shaping behavior the way gravity shapes posture.

✦

This is where the film feels deeply aligned with Tsou's and Baker's ethos: an insistence that marginalized realities don't need translation or spectacle, only attention. The camera doesn't exoticize, doesn't pity, doesn't rush to moral judgment. It stays. It watches people navigate systems that were never designed with them in mind.

And in doing so, it reframes marginalization not as an identity, but as an environment — one that quietly teaches you how much space you're allowed to take up.

✦

What Left-Handed Girl understands is that culture often survives not through force, but through love. Through the desire to belong. Through parents who want better for their children but don't know how to imagine it differently.

Change, in this world, is not revolutionary. It's awkward. It's slow. It's practiced in private.

By the end, nothing is neatly resolved. No speeches. No grand rejection of tradition.

Just a quiet recalibration — a hand allowed to move freely, even if no one says it out loud.
