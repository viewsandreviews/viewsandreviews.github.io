Film Review

Dune: Part Two

The Darker Knight
Director Denis Villeneuve
Year 2024
Starring Timothée Chalamet, Zendaya, Austin Butler

Step aside, Anakin —
we're re-diving into some sand dunes,
and we're gonna love it.

It truly does get everywhere.

Villeneuve brings us back to his new homeland of Arrakis, the desert land of the space Middle East. The planet is now property of none but the local desert people: the superstitious, spice-headed Fremen. The religious survivalists are still uncertain of the rightfulness of their white Messiah, pretty boy Paul Atreides — the one believed to free the Fremen from the chains of their space colonisers, the cruel and bleak Harkonnens.

✦

Part Two focuses on the evolution of his character. A hero descended from the sky for some. A lover for others. A nightmare for himself.

The story of Dune is far from a hero's journey. A descent into madness will be the true voyage — one to rebuild an order that sees no good or evil. That is the vision he sees and accepts for himself. But whether it is a vision of truth and justice will remain unknown.

The superstition of the Fremen is said to come from truth, as all superstition does. But the truth comes from the will of the deceivers. The freemasonry known as the Bene Gesserit — the closest thing to an ultimate villain in this shaded world — has long determined the destiny of the galaxy. Sitting right across from a not-so-God Emperor, they are the ones who whisper the rules of good and evil. Build idols out of men; create destinies out of impermanent universes. Even in defeat, their plans prevail, as victory was never the goal.

✦

Because there is never a victor in this space race for power. The persecuted suffer from persecution, while the ones in power are victims of their own paranoias. This not-so-subtle analogy to the still-modern conflict of ideas reminds us that we may choose where to lay our pins of virtue into the ever-long spectrum of morality — but that pin may look so much different depending on where you look at it from.

That is the ultimate message of Dune's author, Frank Herbert. A message transported only slightly altered by an ever-successful Villeneuve. Herbert and Villeneuve's shared liberal cynicism allows them to keep intact the soul of this classic. We cheer for victims whose hearts are as bleak as the villainous Harkonnens — excused only by ignorance and non-galaxy levels of BDSM extravaganza. The dreams built by lies become prophecies stronger than the will, or reason, of the people.

✦

Dune: Part Two is now the ultimate blockbuster. The Darker Knight. The Empire Strikes Back and Forth. Jurassic Galaxy. Villeneuve is crowned. He now sits alone on the throne that was being claimed by so many.

What the brilliant director delivers is a masterclass of visionary cinematography, with excellence in all other departments. The colours — a blend of intricate monochromatic contrasts, splendid for colour-deficient eyes. The congestion of futuristic industrial designs, alternated by long shots of dreadful deserts and infinite spaces. The gravitas of a soundtrack that comes at the most expected but most needed times. The foam-inducing action. The Worms. The WORMS.

He even turns his heartthrobs into esteemed actors deserving of praise that goes past their performance. His already acclaimed talents allow him to fully express his vision, while still following the penny-picking hand of the big machine — trying to accommodate the taste of the people, adding a blend of flavours that suit an almost complete universe.

✦

Love story building is not Villeneuve's forte, but enough effort is made so that when the final twists are revealed, emotional chain reactions are ensured. Paul Atreides' very progressive Fremen lover, Chani (Zendaya), quickly falls for his sandworm riding skills — just to reciprocate by showcasing her own riding prowess. Enough time is dedicated to the duo, but the chemistry between the two lacks enough… spice.

Villeneuve's trials for humour — very sporadic ones — are mostly remembered for allowing audiences time to cough awkwardly. But these are just small bleeps into a grandiose project that will inevitably strike the diary of cinema, while the popcorn buckets will be filled by the sandy worms of the soulless.
