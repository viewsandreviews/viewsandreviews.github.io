TV Review

The Boys — Season 4

The Planned Tradition That Never Ends
Creator Eric Kripke
Year 2024
Starring Karl Urban, Antony Starr, Jack Quaid

Don't ever do drugs, kids.
Or inject them
in your chin.

There we have it, the planned tradition that never ends. Our biennial reunion with a once favourite gang of misfits, now finally back for round 4. The mates, the dudes — best known as the Boys — are back. Aged, sickened and GMO'd. But they are back.

And just like the chronically ever-more-apart reunions with aged parties of friends, this 4th season underwhelms all initial fantasies and expectations. We pick up from a time of intrigues, betrayals and cliffhangers — just to continue with a flattened lifeline of saddened characters and splashy humour.

✦

The Boys, captained by 21st-century pirate William Butcher, are prepped up more than ever to exterminate the radicalised fascist Supes. With the old squadron of multicoloured humans, they are still accompanied by their own two often underwhelming superhumans: the mewing champion Starlight and the Bechdel test victim Kimiko.

Their adversaries remain the same. A Justice League parody which slowly becomes a parody of itself — The Seven, a group of, you guessed it, four superheroes. Their heroic acts are ever more reduced, as this company of previously circumstantial villains is forcefully transformed into a collection of psycho perverts.

✦

The most psycho of them all, Homelander — initially born as a parody of Superman — slowly becomes a reflection of the US's rehabilitation programmes for the mentally impaired. The breast-milk-sucking mommy boy comes back with a crescendo of insecurities, all of which he can only combat through a show of super-force. What now differs is that the broken heroes of this story must counter the violence with just as much violence of their own.

A few new characters are thrown into the mix, although the vast majority are nothing more than a poor ploy for additional political commentary. The most interesting newly introduced character, Jeffrey Dean Morgan's Joe Kessler, ends up being little more than a fantasy. Instead, the political undertones now sing louder than sopranos, deafening any attempt at reconciliation with a once heterogeneous audience.

✦

Unfortunately that is not the only critique to be levied at the deteriorating show. The Boys are in fact the latest victim of the Rick & Morty effect, where an overload of the absurd and obscene fails to capture the eyes, which are instead turned away by regurgitated plotlines. The appealing novelty of bloody action, which lasted up until previous seasons, is now turned into dullness.

Failing love stories and uninteresting developments do not provide additional support, especially when they consume the majority of screen time and overshadow the more captivating side stories. The overaccumulation of characters self-pitying causes tables to turn. Emotions fail to drive the viewers' empathy; instead, simplicity is the only parameter left.

The big bad villain becomes the only one to follow — at least story-wise, his is the only arc worth pursuing. The deranged and quasi-clownish level of malignity of the archenemy paradoxically becomes a fan favourite, although it is a paradox we have often encountered.

✦

The finale is a saving grace of last-minute excellence, doing the impossible to save the last ounces of interest left for this satire of itself. Now, with one last season to go and several controversial political campaigns to come, what remains to be unfolded is which side of history will reign — which will sadly also determine the final judgement on a supposedly artistic composition.

(Hi)Story repeats. Storytellers redo.
